Ancient Maya

A civilization of astronomers, builders, and storytellers

Who were the Maya?

The Maya civilization flourished across present-day southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador for more than two thousand years. From the earliest settlements around 2000 BC to the dazzling cities of the Classic period (250 AD – 900 AD), the Maya built stone pyramids, palaces, ball courts, and observatories deep in the rainforest.

They were remarkable mathematicians and astronomers. They used the concept of zero, tracked the movements of Venus, and produced a calendar so precise it measured the solar year to within a fraction of a day. Their hieroglyphic writing — one of the few fully-developed scripts of the ancient Americas — recorded the lives of kings, the gods, and the turning of the cosmos.

A timeline of the Maya

  1. Preclassic 2000 BC – 250 AD

    First villages grow into cities. Monumental architecture appears at sites like Nakbe and El Mirador.

  2. Classic 250 AD – 900 AD

    The golden age. Tikal, Palenque, Copan, and Calakmul rise as rival city-states.

  3. Postclassic 900 AD – 1524 AD

    Power shifts north to the Yucatan. Chichen Itza and later Mayapan dominate the peninsula.

  4. Today Present

    More than six million Maya people live in the region, still speaking thirty or more Mayan languages.

Great cities of the Maya

Copan

Honduras · Classic Period

Famous for its intricate stelae and the Hieroglyphic Stairway, which records the longest Maya text ever found in stone.

Calakmul

Mexico · Classic Period

The great rival of Tikal. Hidden deep in the Campeche rainforest, its central pyramid is one of the largest ever built by the Maya.

Chichen Itza

Mexico · Postclassic Period

Home of El Castillo, the pyramid of Kukulkan. Twice a year, the setting sun casts a serpent of shadow down its staircase.

El Caracol

Chichen Itza · Observatory

A round tower with windows aligned to the movements of Venus — proof of the Maya's gift for watching the sky.

Palenque

Mexico · Classic Period

The Temple of the Inscriptions hides the tomb of King Pakal the Great, one of the greatest archaeological discoveries of the 20th century.

Writing in stone

Across every Maya city, kings raised tall carved stones called stelae to mark important dates: births, coronations, victories in battle, and the ends of calendar cycles. Together, these stelae give us a story of the Maya told in their own words.
