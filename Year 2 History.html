Topic 1

Life Long Ago

Life was very different when your grandparents were little.

Some things that are NORMAL now didn’t exist when your grandparents were young:

  • No smartphones or tablets.
  • No internet. Just books and libraries.
  • Televisions had just three channels and the pictures were in black and white.
  • People wrote letters and posted them — no email.
  • You played outside or with toys — no video games.

Some things are the same:

  • School, with teachers, pencils and books.
  • Bicycles, footballs and skipping ropes.
  • Christmas, birthdays, and going on holiday.

Ask a grown-up: “What toys did you play with when you were 7?” You might be surprised!

History words
  • Past — things that have already happened.
  • Present — right now.
  • Future — things yet to come.
  • Year — one trip around the Sun (365 days).
  • Century — 100 years.
Topic 2

The Great Fire of London

In the summer of 1666 — over 350 years ago — London caught fire and burned for four days.

It started in a tiny bakery on Pudding Lane. The baker, Thomas Farriner, went to bed. Something in the oven still smoked, and a spark set the shop alight.

In 1666, most houses in London were made of wood and packed tightly together. The summer had been hot and dry. The fire jumped from roof to roof faster than anyone could put it out.

It burned for four days. By the end:

  • More than 13,000 houses had been destroyed.
  • Old St Paul’s Cathedral was ruined.
  • Most of London inside the city walls was gone.

Luckily, very few people died. After the fire, London was rebuilt with brick and stone, which don’t burn as easily. A tall column called The Monument still stands today to remember the fire.

We know about the fire because a man called Samuel Pepys wrote it all down in his diary.

Key dates
  • 2 September 1666 — fire starts at the bakery.
  • 3–5 September 1666 — fire spreads across London.
  • 6 September 1666 — the fire is finally put out.

Easy to remember: "One, six, six, six — lots of sticks".

Topic 3

Florence Nightingale & Mary Seacole

Two brave nurses who changed how we look after sick people.

Florence Nightingale (1820–1910) was born in Italy to a rich English family. Against her family’s wishes, she trained as a nurse.

During the Crimean War (over 170 years ago), she went to a dirty army hospital where many soldiers were dying — not from wounds, but from germs. Florence:

  • Insisted on clean beds, clean clothes, and clean water.
  • Walked the hospital at night with a lamp, checking on every soldier. They called her “The Lady with the Lamp”.
  • After the war, she set up the first proper nursing school in London.

Mary Seacole (1805–1881) was born in Jamaica. Her mother was a healer who used herbal medicines. Mary learned from her and also worked during the Crimean War.

Mary was told she couldn’t join as a nurse, so she travelled to the war by herself. She set up her own “British Hotel” near the fighting, to care for wounded soldiers. She was very loved by them.

Both women helped us understand that cleanliness saves lives.

Why we remember them

Because of Florence and Mary, hospitals today are clean. Nurses wear clean uniforms, wash their hands carefully, and keep germs away from patients. That saves lives every single day.

Topic 4

Neil Armstrong & the Moon

On 20 July 1969, a person walked on the Moon for the first time ever.

Neil Armstrong was an American astronaut. In 1969 he rode on a huge rocket called Apollo 11, with two other astronauts: Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins.

The rocket flew all the way to the Moon — about 239,000 miles away. A small landing ship called the Eagle came down onto the dusty surface.

When Neil stepped out onto the Moon, he said the famous words:

“That’s one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.”

He and Buzz Aldrin walked around, took photos, picked up Moon rocks, and left an American flag behind. Their footprints are still there today — because the Moon has no wind or rain to wash them away!

Topic 5

Rosa Parks

In 1955, in America, a woman named Rosa changed the world by staying in her seat.

A long time ago in parts of America, there were unfair rules called segregation. Black and white people were forced to live, eat, and travel separately. On a bus, Black people had to sit at the BACK and give up their seat if a white person wanted it.

On 1 December 1955, a Black woman called Rosa Parks was on a bus in Montgomery, Alabama. When the driver ordered her to give up her seat for a white man, she said “No”.

She was arrested. But her quiet brave refusal sparked a huge protest. For over a year, Black people in Montgomery refused to use the buses at all until the rules changed.

Rosa Parks became known as the “Mother of the Civil Rights Movement”. Because of her, and many others, the unfair rules were changed. Today, everyone can sit anywhere on any bus.
