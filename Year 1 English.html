Topic 1

Phonics Sounds

Letters and the sounds they make.

When you first learn to read, you’re doing something amazing. The squiggles on the page — letters — each stand for a sound. Your job is to turn the squiggles into sounds, and then to blend those sounds into whole words. That’s phonics.

Some sounds are just one letter. The letter "c" usually says "kuh". The letter "a" often says "ah". Put them together: c-a-t = cat.

But lots of sounds are TWO letters working as a team. These are called digraphs:

  • sh as in ship, shop, fish
  • ch as in chip, chop, chair
  • th as in thumb, thin, path
  • ng as in ring, sing, long

Once you know the sounds each letter (or pair of letters) makes, you can work out brand new words you’ve never seen before. Just sound it out, one bit at a time, then blend them together. That’s the whole secret of reading.

Topic 2

Tricky Words

Some words you just have to remember.

Most English words can be sounded out, but some sneaky ones can’t. The letters don’t say the sounds you’d expect. We call these tricky words, and the best way to handle them is to learn them by sight.

Take the word the. If you tried to sound it out, you’d get "t-h-e" — something like "thuh-eh". That’s not how we say it. We just know that "the" says "thuh". You have to learn it with your eyes, not your ears.

Other tricky words include:

the, was, his, some, one, are, my, you, said, have, they, there, do, to, of, I, put

A great way to learn tricky words is to write them out, or spot them in books. The more you see a tricky word, the quicker your brain recognises it next time. After a while, you won’t even remember they were tricky.

Topic 3

Capital Letters & Full Stops

Every sentence needs a big letter at the start.

When you write, you want the reader to know where each sentence begins and ends. That’s what capital letters and full stops are for. They’re like little road signs for the reader.

Every sentence STARTS with a CAPITAL letter. Every sentence ENDS with a full stop. These are the two most important rules in Year 1 writing.

Capital letters also sit at the start of names:

  • People: Amir, Rosie, Mum, Dad.
  • Places: London, Paris, the River Thames.
  • Days of the week: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday.
  • Months of the year: January, February, March.
  • The word I when you mean yourself — ALWAYS a capital, even if it’s in the middle of a sentence. This one catches lots of people out.

Instead of a full stop, some sentences end with a question mark (?) if you’re asking something, or an exclamation mark (!) if you’re shouting or feeling excited.

Topic 4

One or Many

Adding -s or -es.

If you have one cat, that’s easy: a cat. But what if you have more than one? You add an s at the end: two cats. That’s called making the word plural (the opposite of singular, which means just one).

For most words, plural means just sticking an s on the end:

  • one dog → two dogs
  • one book → ten books
  • one pencil → five pencils

Some words are a bit different. If a word ends in s, ss, sh, ch, x or z, adding just an "s" sounds weird. Instead we add es:

  • one bus → three buses
  • one box → four boxes
  • one brush → ten brushes

Try saying "buss-es" — it flows. "Buss" just ending in a second "s" sound doesn’t quite work. English has invented all these little rules over the years, and most of them are just because the alternatives sounded ugly!

Topic 5

Reading Stories

Books are full of adventures.

Reading a story is a bit like going on holiday without leaving the sofa. The words on the page let you visit places that don’t even exist, meet characters you’ve never met, and feel what they feel.

Good readers don’t just sound out the words. They picture the story in their head, as if they’re watching a little film. They imagine the colours, the sounds, the smells. When a dragon roars, they hear it. When a character cries, they feel a bit sad too.

Good questions to ask yourself about any story:

  • Who is it about? Name the characters.
  • Where does it happen? In a forest? A castle? Under the sea?
  • What happens at the start, the middle, and the end?
  • How do the people in the story feel? Happy? Scared? Surprised?
  • What do YOU think about the story?

The best way to get better at reading is to read lots. A little bit every day is better than a big chunk once a week. Find books you love — about dinosaurs, princesses, space, football, anything — and the rest follows.
