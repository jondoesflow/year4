Topic 1

Counting to 20 and beyond

Numbers are like friends in a line.

Counting is the very first thing we do in maths. It sounds simple, but counting well is the base of almost everything else.

In Year 1 we count up to 20 and back, and later up to 100. We start with one, and we add one more each time. Try it out loud: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty.

Counting backwards is just as important. Start at ten and go down: ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one, zero. Zero means "none". If you eat all your grapes, you have zero grapes left.

We also start writing number words. The first ten are worth learning because they appear everywhere.

Number words 1 to 10
  • 1 one
  • 2 two
  • 3 three
  • 4 four
  • 5 five
  • 6 six
  • 7 seven
  • 8 eight
  • 9 nine
  • 10 ten

Once you know these by heart, bigger numbers are much easier.

Topic 2

Adding small numbers

Adding means putting groups together.

Adding is what we do when we bring two groups of things together to make one bigger group. Your parents do it when they work out the shopping bill. You do it when you share sweets and want to know how many there are altogether.

Picture three apples on the table. Someone brings you two more. How many do you have now? Let’s count them: one, two, three, four, five. Five apples. We write this as 3 + 2 = 5. The plus sign (+) means "and", and the equals sign (=) means "is the same as".

A good trick for adding is to count on. Start with the bigger number and add the smaller one by counting up. For 4 + 3, hold the 4 in your head and count: 5, 6, 7. You land on seven. That’s the answer.

Your fingers are a brilliant first calculator. If you’re adding two small numbers, use your fingers. Nothing fancy needed.

Topic 3

Taking away

Subtracting means taking some away.

Taking away is the opposite of adding. When you eat one of your five grapes, you have fewer grapes than before. You have taken one away.

Say you start with five sweets. You eat two. How many are left? Count out loud: "I had five. I ate one, so now four. I ate another, so now three." You have three sweets left. We write this as 5 − 2 = 3. The minus sign (−) means "take away".

Just like counting on helps with adding, counting back helps with subtracting. For 8 − 3, start at 8 and count back three: 7, 6, 5. You land on five.

Adding and subtracting are opposites. If you know that 3 + 2 = 5, you also know that 5 − 2 = 3. Every addition sum has a matching subtraction sum. Maths is full of these patterns once you start looking for them.

Topic 4

2D shapes

Flat shapes to draw on paper.

Look around your room right now. Can you spot a square? A rectangle? A circle? Shapes are everywhere. Pictures, books, windows, doors, phones, plates. Once you know the names, you’ll see them all the time.

A flat shape is one you can draw on paper. We call these 2D shapes. The most important ones to know in Year 1 are:

  • Circle — perfectly round, with no corners or straight edges at all. Like a coin, a clock face, or the moon.
  • Triangle — has 3 sides and 3 corners. Like a slice of pizza or a road sign.
  • Square — has 4 sides, all the same length. Like a crisps packet or a dice face.
  • Rectangle — has 4 sides, but two are long and two are short. Like a door, a phone screen, or a cereal box.

Count the corners and count the sides. That’s always how to check what shape you’re looking at. The corners are called vertices (one of them is a vertex) — that’s a tricky word but worth knowing.

Topic 5

Coins and pennies

The coins we use every day.

Money is the clever thing we use to swap for other things. When you go to the shop and want a bar of chocolate, you can’t just take it. You give the shopkeeper money, and she gives you the chocolate. That’s how it works.

In the UK, we have coins worth 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 and £2. The “p” stands for pence. The “£” stands for pound. One pound is worth exactly 100 pence, so a chocolate bar that costs 100p is the same as one that costs £1.

In Year 1 we practise recognising all these coins and making simple amounts. For example, five 2p coins make 10p. Two 5p coins also make 10p. There are lots of ways to make the same amount.

We also practise working out change. If something costs 7p and you pay 10p, how many pennies should you get back? 3p. You’re already doing real-world maths.
