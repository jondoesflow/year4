Topic 1

Place Value to 1,000

Reading and writing numbers up to one thousand.

In Year 2 you worked with numbers up to 100. In Year 3 we triple it and start working with numbers up to 1,000. That’s ten times more than before — enough to count most of the children in your school, or the pages of a thick book.

The secret to big numbers is understanding place value. Every digit sits in a column that tells you exactly what it’s worth. In a three-digit number there are three columns: hundreds, tens and ones.

Take 426. Reading from the right: the 6 is in the ones column, so it means just six. The 2 is in the tens column, so it means 20 (two tens). The 4 is in the hundreds column, so it means 400 (four hundreds). Add them back up: 400 + 20 + 6 = 426. That’s how all three-digit numbers work.

We also practise counting in 50s and 100s. Try it: 50, 100, 150, 200, 250… Or in hundreds: 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000. Both of these are really useful when you’re working out how far away a thousand is from a number.

Partition numbers

487 = 400 + 80 + 7

629 = 600 + 20 + 9

705 = 700 + 0 + 5

Partitioning makes adding and subtracting much easier.

Topic 2

Adding & Subtracting

Column method with 3-digit numbers.

With numbers this big, counting on your fingers stops being practical. Imagine trying to add 326 and 145 by counting one-by-one! Instead, we use the column method, which is the grown-up way that works every time.

Here’s how it works: line up the two numbers with the ones on top of the ones, the tens on top of the tens, the hundreds on top of the hundreds. Then work from right to left. Add the ones first. Then the tens. Then the hundreds.

Tricky bit: what if the ones column adds up to more than 9? For 326 + 145, the ones are 6 + 5 = 11. You can’t write 11 in one column, so you write the 1 and carry the 10 over to the tens column as a little 1 on top. Then when you add the tens (2 + 4 + the carried 1), you get 7. That gives you 471.

Subtraction works the same way but in reverse. If you can’t take the bottom digit away from the top one in a column, you borrow from the next column to the left.

Always check your work by doing the opposite sum. If 326 + 145 = 471, then 471 − 145 should equal 326. If it doesn’t, you know you’ve made a mistake somewhere.

Topic 3

3, 4 and 8 Times Tables

Three new tables to learn by heart.

Times tables are maths facts you want to know instantly, without having to work them out. The quicker you know them, the easier every other bit of maths becomes. In Year 2 you learned the 2, 5 and 10 times tables. In Year 3, three more join the party: 3, 4 and 8.

Here’s the clever thing. The 4 times table is just double the 2 times table. If you know 2 × 6 = 12, then 4 × 6 must be double that: 24. And the 8 times table is just double the 4 times table. 4 × 6 = 24, so 8 × 6 = 48.

The 3 times table is slightly trickier because there’s no easy doubling shortcut. You can count up in 3s: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 33, 36. Saying it aloud is a good way to get it stuck in your head. A lot of people learn times tables by singing them.

Remember this important rule: 3 × 4 is the same as 4 × 3. The order you multiply in doesn’t change the answer. So if you can’t remember one, try the other. You might know 4 × 6 but not 6 × 4 — same sum, same answer.

Topic 4

Fractions

Halves, quarters, thirds and tenths.

A fraction is just a way to talk about part of a whole thing. If you share a pizza between four people, each person gets one quarter. If you share a chocolate bar between two people, each gets one half. Fractions are everywhere in real life.

Every fraction has two numbers and a line. The number on the bottom is called the denominator. It tells you how many equal pieces the whole is cut into. The number on top is called the numerator. It tells you how many of those pieces you have.

So 3/4 means: the whole is cut into 4 equal pieces, and you have 3 of them. Eaten three-quarters of a pizza? You’ve had 3 of the 4 slices.

Equivalent fractions are fractions that LOOK different but mean exactly the same amount. 1/2 is the same as 2/4, which is the same as 5/10. Cut a cake into 2 and take 1 piece — you have 1/2. Cut the same cake into 4 and take 2 pieces — you’ve still got half a cake. Same thing, different slicing.

Year 3 also introduces tenths — sharing into 10 equal parts. 3/10 is three tenths; 7/10 is seven tenths. Tenths will be very useful when we start using decimals.

Topic 5

Measurement & Time

Lengths, weights, clocks and money.

Measuring is how we answer questions like “how long is it?”, “how heavy is it?”, “how much does it hold?” and “how long does it take?”. Different questions need different units.

For length, we use:

  • Millimetres (mm) for tiny things, like the thickness of a coin. 10 mm = 1 cm.
  • Centimetres (cm) for small things, like your pencil. 100 cm = 1 m.
  • Metres (m) for rooms and people. 1,000 m = 1 km.
  • Kilometres (km) for long distances, like the gap between towns.

For weight: 1,000 grams (g) make 1 kilogram (kg). For capacity (how much something holds): 1,000 millilitres (ml) make 1 litre.

For time, Year 3 practises reading clocks to the nearest minute — not just o’clock and half past. We also work in 12-hour time (“3 o’clock in the afternoon”) and 24-hour time (“15:00” on a station display).

And for money: we add and subtract amounts in pounds and pence. 100 pence (100p) = £1. If something costs £2.45 and you pay with a £5 note, you get £2.55 change.

Topic 6

Shape & Angles

Right angles and the turns that make them.

Angles are all about turns. When you turn around to face the other way, that’s an angle. When a door swings open, that’s an angle. When a clock’s hand moves, that’s an angle. Even if you didn’t know it, you’ve been making angles all your life.

The most important angle in Year 3 is the right angle. It’s a quarter of a full turn — like turning to face your neighbour in a line. It looks like a perfect square corner: the corner of a book, the corner of a door, the corner of a table. Rectangles and squares have four right angles each.

If you make four right angles in a row, you’ve done a full turn all the way round — 360 degrees. Two right angles make a half turn (a straight line, 180 degrees). One right angle is a quarter turn (90 degrees). Three right angles is a three-quarter turn (270 degrees).

We also measure the perimeter of shapes. That’s the total distance around the outside. If you walked all the way round the edge of a rectangle, how far would you have walked? That’s its perimeter. You just add up all the side lengths.
