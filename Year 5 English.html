Topic 1

Relative Clauses

Adding extra information to a noun.

Writers are always looking for neat ways to pack more information into a sentence without making it clunky. One of the best tools for this is the relative clause.

A relative clause is a group of words that adds extra information about a noun. It usually starts with one of these relative pronouns: who, which, that, whose, whom, where, or when.

Look at this plain sentence: The girl is my cousin. Now let’s add a relative clause to tell you more about the girl: The girl who lives next door is my cousin. See how much more we know?

More examples to watch the pattern:

  • The book that Sam gave me was brilliant.
  • The shop, which opened last week, sells amazing cakes.
  • That’s the teacher whose dog won the prize.
  • Do you remember the day when we went camping?

There’s one important punctuation rule. If the relative clause is EXTRA (the sentence would still make sense without it), wrap it in commas. If it’s ESSENTIAL (the sentence needs it to make sense), no commas needed. Compare: “The teacher, who lives near me, gave me a lift.” (extra info) vs. “The teacher who lives near me gave me a lift.” (essential — there are lots of teachers, we’re specifying which one).

Topic 2

Modal Verbs

Words that show how likely or possible something is.

Modal verbs are little helper verbs that change the meaning of another verb. They tell you whether something is possible, necessary, allowed, or just a suggestion. They never change their shape — no “-s” on the end, no past tense version of their own.

The main modal verbs are: can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would.

Each one adds a different flavour to the verb it’s helping:

  • I can swim. — I’m able to.
  • I could swim when I was five. — I was able to.
  • You may leave early. — You’re allowed to.
  • You might see a fox. — It’s possible.
  • You must wear a coat. — It’s necessary.
  • We should leave soon. — It’s a good idea.
  • It will rain later. — It’s going to happen.
  • I would love that. — If I could, I’d want to.

Notice the subtle differences. “You must wear a coat” sounds stricter than “You should wear a coat”. “It will rain” is more certain than “It might rain”. Modal verbs are how we turn the dial on our certainty and politeness.

Topic 3

Parenthesis & Brackets

Adding extra information, the neat way.

Parenthesis is a posh word for extra information you drop into a sentence. The clever thing about parenthesis is that you can REMOVE it and the sentence still works. It’s optional flavour, not the main course.

You have three ways to mark parenthesis in writing:

  • Brackets (like these): My cat (who is called Patch) is very fluffy.
  • Dashes — like these —: My cat — who is called Patch — is very fluffy.
  • Commas, like these,: My cat, who is called Patch, is very fluffy.

All three give exactly the same meaning. They differ in feel: brackets are the quietest (the reader barely notices), dashes feel the most dramatic, and commas are the most common in everyday writing.

The test: if you remove the parenthesis, does the sentence still make sense on its own? “My cat is very fluffy.” Yes. Good — that means it was parenthesis, and you used the marks correctly.

Parenthesis is really useful for extra details that add colour without getting in the way of the main point. Use it when you want to be informative without being long-winded.

Topic 4

Cohesion

Joining ideas smoothly.

Ever read something where the ideas jump around like the writer couldn’t decide what to say? Or where every sentence starts the same way? That’s writing with poor cohesion. Cohesion means how well a piece of writing hangs together.

Good writers use three main tools to make their writing flow:

Adverbials of time, place and manner help guide the reader:

  • Time: First, Next, Afterwards, Later, Meanwhile, Finally, The following morning, Eventually.
  • Place: Nearby, On the other side, In the distance, Below.
  • Manner: Without warning, Suddenly, Cautiously, Bit by bit.

Pronouns avoid repetition. Instead of “Lily took the lead. Lily ran fast.”, we write: “Lily took the lead. She ran fast.” The pronoun “she” still refers clearly back to Lily, and the writing sounds better.

Synonyms and variety keep writing fresh. If you’ve written “the castle” four times in a paragraph, try alternatives: the fortress, the stronghold, the stone walls, its ancient halls.

Here’s a worked example. Bad: Lily ran fast. Ravi ran behind Lily. Lily was the fastest. Better (with cohesion): Lily took the lead. She ran so fast that, within moments, Ravi was far behind.

Topic 5

Formal & Informal

Different styles for different readers.

The way you write should match who you’re writing TO. You wouldn’t speak to a head teacher the same way you speak to a best friend, and writing works the same way. We talk about register — the style level of writing.

Informal writing is relaxed and chatty. It’s for friends and family. Texts, quick emails to your mum, your own diary. In informal writing, you can use:

  • Contractions: don’t, can’t, I’ve, it’s.
  • Slang and chatty phrases: loads of fun, kind of weird, sort of hungry.
  • Exclamation marks for excitement.
  • A personal voice: I think, I reckon, I can’t believe.

Formal writing is tidy, polite and neutral. It’s for letters to strangers, school reports, newspaper articles, emails to your teacher, or applications. In formal writing you should:

  • Use FULL words: do not (not don’t), it is (not it’s).
  • Avoid slang and chatty words.
  • Use polite, precise vocabulary: request, require, approximately, therefore, however.
  • Use passive voice more often: “The letter was delivered” rather than “Someone delivered the letter”.

Compare: “Hiya! Just wondering if you could send me the form?” (informal) vs. “Dear Sir or Madam, Please could you send me the form? Many thanks,” (formal). Both work, but for different readers.

Topic 6

Year 5 Tricky Words

Words that trip lots of people up.

Year 5 is where English gets serious. The Department for Education publishes a statutory list of words that every Year 5 and 6 pupil should be able to spell — and they’re properly tricky. A lot of adults get them wrong too.

Here are some of the tough ones from the Year 5 list:

accommodate, achieve, ancient, apparent, attached, available, bargain, bruise, category, cemetery, committee, community, desperate, determined, develop, dictionary, disastrous, embarrass, environment, equipment, especially, exaggerate, excellent, existence, explanation, familiar, foreign, forty, frequently, government, guarantee, harass, hindrance, identity, immediate, individual, interfere, interrupt, language, leisure, lightning, marvellous, mischievous, muscle, necessary, neighbour, nuisance, occupy, occur, opportunity, parliament, persuade, physical, prejudice, privilege, profession, programme, pronunciation, queue, recognise, recommend, relevant, restaurant, rhyme, rhythm, sacrifice, secretary, shoulder, signature, sincere, soldier, stomach, sufficient, suggest, symbol, system, temperature, thorough, twelfth, variety, vegetable, vehicle, yacht

Strategies for learning these:

  • Look, say, cover, write, check. Write the word. Look at it carefully. Say it out loud. Cover it up. Write it from memory. Uncover and check.
  • Spot the pattern. Necessary has one C and two Ss. Committee has two Ms, two Ts, two Es. Find a memorable fact about each tough word.
  • Mnemonics. Necessary = “Never Eat Chips, Eat Salad Sandwiches And Remain Young.”
  • Notice them in books. The more you see a word in the wild, the quicker your brain locks it in.
