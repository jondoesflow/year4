Topic 1

The Stone Age

Life before metal.

For most of human history, there was no metal. No cars, no writing, no castles. People lived off the land, using tools made of whatever was lying around. The time before metal is called the Stone Age, and it’s the longest chunk of human history by far — it lasted from about 2.5 million years ago all the way to around 4,500 years ago.

Historians split the Stone Age into three periods:

  • Palaeolithic (the Old Stone Age). People were hunter-gatherers — they hunted wild animals and gathered berries, roots and nuts. They used fire, painted pictures on cave walls, and made tools from flint (a hard rock they chipped into sharp edges).
  • Mesolithic (the Middle Stone Age). Ice Ages ended and the climate warmed. People made smaller, neater tools and lived in small camps that moved with the seasons.
  • Neolithic (the New Stone Age). This is the HUGE moment. People stopped wandering and started farming. They kept animals like cows and sheep, grew wheat, and built the first villages. They also made pottery, and built amazing stone monuments.

The most famous Neolithic monument in Britain is Stonehenge, out on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire. It was built about 5,000 years ago. Those huge stones were dragged from miles away, without wheels, by hundreds of people working together. Nobody is completely sure why it was built — it might have been a giant calendar for tracking the Sun, a burial site, or both.

Topic 2

The Bronze Age

People learn to make metal.

Around 2,500 BC, somebody in the Middle East made a discovery that changed everything. They found that if you melt down two soft metals — copper and tin — and mix them in the right amounts, you get a brand new metal called bronze. Bronze is much harder than either of its ingredients. It holds a sharp edge and it doesn’t break easily.

Suddenly, you could have bronze axes, bronze knives, bronze spears and bronze jewellery. All far better than stone. The knowledge slowly spread across Europe and reached Britain. The Bronze Age in Britain lasted from about 2500 BC to 800 BC.

Bronze Age people in Britain:

  • Lived in round houses with thatched roofs made of reeds or straw. A family all slept in one room around a fire in the middle.
  • Kept cattle and sheep, and grew wheat and barley.
  • Buried their important dead with their valuables in burial mounds called barrows. You can still see these big grassy lumps dotted across the English countryside today.
  • Made jewellery from gold and bronze. Some of the necklaces found in Bronze Age graves are stunning.
Topic 3

The Iron Age

Iron tools, hillforts and tribes.

Bronze was a big upgrade from stone, but iron was an even bigger upgrade from bronze. Iron is harder, and the ore needed to make it (iron rock) is much more common than tin. Once people worked out how to smelt iron, it gradually replaced bronze.

The Iron Age in Britain ran from about 800 BC to 43 AD — right up until the Romans arrived.

Iron Age Britons lived very differently to us:

  • They lived in tribes, each ruled by its own chief (a kind of local king). Different parts of Britain were ruled by different tribes.
  • They built hillforts — walled settlements on top of hills, with huge earth banks and ditches around them for defence. You can still visit many of them today, like Maiden Castle in Dorset.
  • They were skilled farmers, growing crops in fields and keeping animals.
  • They made beautiful jewellery, pottery, and weapons, often decorated with swirling patterns.

Iron Age religion was complicated. The religious leaders and wise people were called Druids. They didn’t write anything down — they passed their knowledge on by memory and word of mouth. That means we still don’t fully understand what they believed.

Topic 4

Ancient Egypt

Pharaohs, pyramids and the River Nile.

While stone-age people were still roaming Britain, in North Africa an astonishing civilisation was already flourishing. Ancient Egypt grew up along the River Nile, and it lasted for over 3,000 years — longer than any other civilisation you can think of. It started around 3100 BC.

The Nile was the secret to Egypt’s success. Every year the river flooded, leaving behind thick black mud that was perfect for growing crops. Beyond the river, it was just desert. So all of life in Egypt was squeezed into a narrow green strip along the water.

Egypt was ruled by kings called pharaohs. Egyptians believed pharaohs were actually gods on Earth. When a pharaoh died, they weren’t buried in the ground like ordinary people. They were placed inside enormous stone buildings called pyramids, with all the treasures they’d need in the afterlife. The Great Pyramid at Giza was built about 4,500 years ago and it’s still standing today.

The most famous pharaoh of all is probably Tutankhamun — a boy king who ruled around 1325 BC. He died at only 18. His tomb was found almost completely untouched by an archaeologist called Howard Carter in 1922. Inside were gold masks, chariots, thrones and jewellery. It was the greatest archaeological discovery of the 20th century.

Egyptians wrote in hieroglyphs — picture-writing where each little symbol stands for a sound or an idea. For a long time no one could read them. Then in 1799, French soldiers in Egypt found the Rosetta Stone, which had the same text in three scripts. That let scholars finally crack the code.

Topic 5

A Big Timeline

Seeing it all in order.

When you’re learning history, it really helps to see the order of things on a timeline. A lot of stuff that feels ancient and long-ago was actually happening at the SAME TIME as other stuff you’ve heard of. Let’s line up the Year 3 dates:

  • ~2.5 million years BC — the Stone Age begins.
  • ~10,000 BC — Neolithic begins; people start to farm.
  • ~3,100 BC — Ancient Egypt begins along the Nile.
  • ~3,000 BC — Stonehenge is built in Britain.
  • ~2,500 BC — Bronze Age starts in Britain. The Great Pyramid at Giza is built in Egypt.
  • ~800 BC — Iron Age starts in Britain.
  • ~1,325 BC — Tutankhamun reigns in Egypt.
  • 43 AD — Romans invade Britain. The Iron Age ends.

Notice something? When Stonehenge was being built, Ancient Egypt was already a successful civilisation with pyramids. Weird to think about, isn’t it? History doesn’t happen in a neat line — it happens all over the world at once, and different places are at different stages at the same time.
