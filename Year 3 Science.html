Topic 1

Plants

How a plant actually works.

Plants seem so simple — they just sit there being green — but they’re actually brilliant little food factories. Every single plant is quietly turning sunlight into food, day in, day out. Nothing else on Earth can do this.

Each part of a plant has a specific job:

  • Roots stretch down into the soil. They hold the plant firmly in the ground so it doesn’t blow over, and they suck up water and tiny nutrients.
  • Stem is the tube that holds the plant up straight. Water travels UP the stem from the roots, while food made in the leaves travels DOWN the stem to everywhere else.
  • Leaves are the food factories. They use sunlight (plus water and a gas called carbon dioxide) to make the plant’s food. This amazing process is called photosynthesis.
  • Flower is the plant’s way of making more plants. It makes seeds after being pollinated, usually by bees or other insects.

The life cycle: a seed is planted. It grows roots downward and a shoot upward. The shoot opens into leaves. Leaves make food. The plant grows taller. Eventually it makes flowers. Bees visit the flowers and pollinate them. The flowers turn into fruit. Inside the fruit are new seeds. Those new seeds fall to the ground, or get eaten by animals and dropped somewhere else, and the whole thing starts again. That cycle has been running for hundreds of millions of years.

Topic 2

Animals and Humans

Skeletons, muscles and nutrition.

Animals, including you, can’t make their own food like plants can. We have to eat other living things — either plants (if we’re herbivores), other animals (if we’re carnivores), or both (if we’re omnivores, like most humans).

Good food gives us energy to run about and building blocks to grow and repair our bodies. To stay healthy, we need the right kinds and amounts of food. Too much sugar, too much fat, not enough vegetables — and our bodies don’t work as well.

Now, about your skeleton. Your body has around 206 bones. Together they do three amazing jobs:

  • Shape — without bones, you’d be a floppy blob.
  • Protection — your skull is a bony helmet for your brain. Your ribs form a cage around your heart and lungs. Your spine protects the bundle of nerves that runs from your brain down your back.
  • Movement — bones give your muscles something to pull on.

Muscles are the stretchy bits that make you move. They work in pairs. Put your hand on your upper arm and bend your elbow — you’ll feel one muscle (your biceps) tightening, while the one on the underside (your triceps) relaxes. Then straighten your arm, and the pair swap over. That’s how all movement works: one muscle pulls, the opposite one relaxes.

Topic 3

Rocks & Fossils

Three kinds of rock, and how fossils form.

Rocks might look like the most boring things on Earth, but geologists (rock scientists) can read them like a history book. Every rock tells a story about how it was made, sometimes hundreds of millions of years ago.

All the rocks on our planet fall into three groups:

  • Sedimentary rocks are made from layers of sand, mud and tiny shells squashed together for millions of years. Sandstone and limestone are sedimentary. Look closely at these and you can often see the layers. Fossils are nearly always found in sedimentary rock.
  • Igneous rocks are made when red-hot lava (melted rock from volcanoes) cools and hardens. Granite and basalt are igneous. They’re usually very hard.
  • Metamorphic rocks started as one kind of rock, then got squashed or heated so much they changed into something else. Marble started as limestone. Slate started as mud that turned into shale, then got squeezed into slate. The word "metamorphic" means "changed shape".

A fossil is the stony remains of a plant or animal that died a very long time ago. When a creature dies and gets buried in mud, over millions of years minerals seep in and turn the bones (or the imprint of the body) into rock. Fossils are how we know dinosaurs existed. Without them, we’d have no idea!

Topic 4

Light & Shadows

We see things because light bounces off them.

Here’s a strange thought: you don’t actually see objects directly. What you see is LIGHT that has bounced off those objects and travelled into your eyes. If there’s no light, you can’t see anything at all. Try switching all the lights off at night and look around — black.

Light comes from light sources. The biggest one by far is the Sun. On Earth we also have: lamps, candles, torches, fires, TV screens, phones, fireflies. These all make their own light. Mirrors, by the way, don’t make light — they just bounce it around very well.

A shadow forms when something blocks the path of light. The object’s shape makes a dark patch on the other side of it. The closer you stand to a light, the bigger your shadow. The further away, the smaller. Try it with a torch at home.

Some materials behave differently with light:

  • Transparent — you can see straight through them. Glass, water, clear plastic.
  • Translucent — some light gets through but the picture is fuzzy. Frosted glass, thin paper.
  • Opaque — no light gets through at all. Wood, metal, you, a book.
  • Reflective — bounces light very well. Mirrors, shiny metal, still water.
Topic 5

Forces & Magnets

Push, pull, and the strange power of magnets.

A force is just a fancy word for a push or a pull. Every single movement in the world is caused by a force of some kind. You pushed open the door. Gravity pulled the apple out of the tree. The wind pushed the boat across the water.

Magnets are amazing because they can push and pull things without even touching them. Hold a magnet near some drawing pins and WHIP — the pins jump across the table and stick. That’s an invisible force called magnetism.

Magnets only attract certain metals:

  • YES: iron, steel, nickel, cobalt.
  • NO: plastic, wood, rubber, glass, paper, aluminium, gold, copper, silver.

So a magnet picks up drawing pins (steel) but not a plastic ruler or a pound coin.

Every magnet has two ends called poles — the North pole and the South pole. Put two magnets near each other and you’ll notice something clever:

  • Opposite poles attract: N and S pull towards each other.
  • Same poles repel: N and N push AWAY from each other. Same with S and S.

Fun fact: the whole Earth acts like a giant magnet. That’s how a compass works — its little magnetic needle is pulled to point at the Earth’s North.
