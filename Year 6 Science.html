Topic 1

Classification

Sorting living things into groups.

There are millions of different species of living things on Earth, and new ones are still being discovered every year. How do scientists make sense of them all? By sorting them into groups — a huge filing system called classification.

The biggest groups are the five kingdoms of life:

  1. Animals — all eat other living things and most can move. Includes you, beetles, whales, jellyfish.
  2. Plants — make their own food using sunlight (photosynthesis). Trees, grass, flowers, seaweed.
  3. Fungi — mushrooms, mould, yeast. Not plants (they can’t do photosynthesis) but not animals either. They absorb food from dead or rotting stuff around them.
  4. Protists — tiny single-celled creatures that don’t fit in the other groups. Amoebas, algae.
  5. Bacteria — the smallest and oldest living things. Most are invisible to the naked eye. A pinch of soil has billions in it.

Within animals, there’s a further split:

  • Vertebrates have a backbone: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals.
  • Invertebrates don’t: insects, spiders, worms, snails, crabs, jellyfish.

To give species unique names, scientists use the Linnaean system, invented by Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus in the 1700s. Every species gets a two-word Latin name. Humans are Homo sapiens. Domestic cats are Felis catus. Lions are Panthera leo. The Latin names are the same in every country, so scientists everywhere know they’re talking about the same thing.

Topic 2

The Circulatory System

How blood travels round your body.

Right now, inside your chest, your heart is beating. It’s been doing that since before you were born, 60 to 100 times every minute, without stopping. Over 80 years, a human heart beats roughly 3 billion times. It’s an astonishing machine.

The heart is the pump at the centre of your circulatory system — the network that delivers oxygen and food to every single one of your cells. The parts of the system:

  • Heart — a muscular pump roughly the size of your fist.
  • Arteries — large blood vessels that carry OXYGEN-RICH blood AWAY from the heart to the rest of the body.
  • Veins — carry blood BACK to the heart (it’s lower in oxygen now because the cells used it).
  • Capillaries — tiny, tiny vessels, only one cell thick. This is where oxygen and nutrients actually pass through the walls into your body’s cells.
  • Blood — the delivery liquid. Red because of the iron in it.

When you exercise, your muscles work harder and need more oxygen. Your heart beats faster and your breathing speeds up to pump more oxygenated blood to the muscles. That’s why you’re out of breath after running.

Things that HARM your circulatory system: smoking (coats your lungs, damages blood vessels), too much sugar and fat over time, sitting still for years on end. Things that HELP: regular exercise, fruit and vegetables, not smoking, enough sleep. Year 6 often covers this because habits started young make the biggest difference.

Topic 3

Evolution & Inheritance

How living things change over millions of years.

Look at a dog and a wolf. They look alike. In fact they are very close relatives — pretty much the same species. Over thousands of years, humans have bred dogs with friendly traits, giving us the hundreds of different dog breeds today. That’s a tiny version of something that happens naturally, over much longer timescales, to every living thing.

Parents pass on characteristics to their children through tiny instruction codes called genes. Genes decide your eye colour, your hair colour, your height range, the shape of your nose. You inherited half of your genes from each parent, which is why you might have your mum’s eyes and your dad’s chin.

Evolution is the slow change in a species over generations. Here’s how it works:

  1. Every animal is slightly different from its parents (gene mixing, tiny random changes).
  2. Some differences help an animal survive and have more babies. Others don’t.
  3. Helpful traits get passed on more. Unhelpful traits die out.
  4. Over thousands of generations, the species SHIFTS.

This is natural selection. It was explained by Charles Darwin in his 1859 book On the Origin of Species. Darwin had sailed round the world on HMS Beagle and noticed that finches on different Galapagos islands had different beak shapes — each matched the food available on their island. Over generations, the finches had evolved to suit their home.

Fossils are our evidence. Fossils from millions of years ago show creatures very different from today. We can trace how whales evolved from land mammals, how birds evolved from dinosaurs, how humans share ancestors with apes.

Topic 4

Light (in more detail)

How we see, and why light behaves oddly.

Light travels in straight lines, very fast (300,000 km per second — fast enough to circle the Earth seven times in one second). We see objects because light bounces off them and into our eyes. Without light, no seeing.

Interesting things light can do:

  • Reflection. Light bounces off smooth surfaces. Mirrors reflect so smoothly that you see a perfect image of yourself. Water, shiny metal and polished glass also reflect.
  • Refraction. Light bends when it passes from one material into another (like from air into water). That’s why a straw in a glass of water looks snapped, why a stick in a river looks broken, why a pool looks shallower than it really is.

Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light. The shape of the shadow copies the shape of the object. The closer the light source, the bigger and blurrier the shadow. Move the source further away and the shadow gets sharper and closer to actual size.

And now for something really cool: rainbows. White sunlight isn’t really one colour at all — it’s a mix of every colour of the rainbow. When sunlight passes through water droplets (in the sky after rain, or through a glass prism), the droplets bend the different colours by different amounts, splitting them apart. You see them as a band of SEVEN colours:

Red · Orange · Yellow · Green · Blue · Indigo · Violet

Remember the order with: “Richard Of York Gave Battle In Vain.” Each word’s first letter matches a colour. (It’s a reference to Richard III, who lost the Battle of Bosworth in 1485, ending the Wars of the Roses. Handy for remembering both rainbows and Tudor history.)

Topic 5

Electricity (in more detail)

Circuit symbols and what changes the brightness.

Electricity is the flow of tiny particles called electrons through a wire. The electrons can only flow if there’s a complete loop — a circuit. Break the loop anywhere, and the flow stops.

In Year 6, we draw circuits using standard symbols that electricians and scientists use all over the world. They’re like a shared visual language:

  • Cell (battery) — drawn as a long line (positive) and a short line (negative).
  • Bulb — a circle with a cross inside.
  • Switch — a gap in the wire that can be opened (broken circuit, nothing flows) or closed (complete, current flows).
  • Wires — just straight lines.
  • Motor — a circle with the letter M.
  • Buzzer — a half-moon shape.

Things that change how brightly a bulb shines:

  • More cells in series (batteries stacked end to end) = more push, brighter bulb.
  • More bulbs in series = the power has to spread between them, each one dimmer.
  • Longer wires = slightly more resistance, slightly dimmer. The effect is small in classroom circuits.

Two different types of circuit:

  • Series circuit — everything is on one loop. If one bulb fails, the whole chain goes out (like cheap Christmas lights).
  • Parallel circuit — components are on separate branches. Each branch gets the full power, and one failing doesn’t break the others (like the lights in your home).
