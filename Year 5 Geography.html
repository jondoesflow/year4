Topic 1

North America

The continent across the Atlantic.

North America stretches from the icy Arctic north all the way down to the warm waters of the Caribbean. It has three giant countries and more than twenty smaller ones in Central America and the Caribbean islands.

The big three:

  • Canada (capital: Ottawa). The second-largest country in the whole world, after Russia. Most Canadians live in a narrow strip near the US border because the rest is too cold or too remote. Famous for forests, lakes, maple syrup and very polite people.
  • USA (capital: Washington, D.C.). The most populated and most powerful country in North America. Don’t confuse the capital with the biggest city — New York is far bigger, but it’s not the capital.
  • Mexico (capital: Mexico City). Once home to the Aztec and Mayan civilisations, Mexico is south of the USA. Mexico City is one of the biggest cities on Earth, built on the site of the ancient Aztec capital.

Famous physical features to know:

  • The Rocky Mountains run up the western side, from New Mexico all the way into Canada.
  • The Great Lakes sit between the USA and Canada. Five lakes so big that sailors treat them like seas.
  • The Mississippi River flows right down the middle of the USA from Minnesota to the Gulf of Mexico.
  • The Grand Canyon in Arizona is a staggeringly deep canyon cut by the Colorado River over millions of years.
Topic 2

South America

The continent of the Amazon and the Andes.

South America hangs below North America like a teardrop. It has 12 countries, most of which speak Spanish (the major exception is Brazil, which speaks Portuguese).

The biggest countries:

  • Brazil (capital: Brasília). The largest country in South America and the fifth-largest in the world. Home to most of the Amazon rainforest. Famous for football, carnival, and beaches.
  • Argentina (capital: Buenos Aires). Famous for tango, beef, and football legends like Maradona and Messi.
  • Peru (capital: Lima). Home to Machu Picchu and the remains of the Inca civilisation.
  • Colombia (capital: Bogotá). Famous for coffee and emeralds.

Famous features — South America is a land of extremes:

  • The Andes run down the entire western coast — the longest mountain range in the world, nearly 7,000 km end to end.
  • The Amazon rainforest is the largest rainforest on Earth. It covers parts of nine countries and is home to more different kinds of plant and animal than anywhere else on the planet.
  • The Amazon River carries more water than any other river, by a huge margin. Almost 20% of all the fresh water flowing into all the world’s oceans comes from the Amazon.
  • Machu Picchu is an amazing 500-year-old Inca city hidden high in the Peruvian Andes. It was forgotten for centuries until an American explorer found it in 1911.
Topic 3

Biomes & Climate Zones

The big regions of the natural world.

A biome is a huge region of the world where the climate, plants and animals all share the same basic character. The Amazon and the Congo are on opposite sides of the Earth, but they’re both tropical rainforest — they have the same hot-and-wet climate and broadly similar plants and animals. That’s what makes them the same biome.

The main biomes of the world:

  • Tropical rainforest — hot all year, with heavy rain. Tall trees with huge leaves. Home to the highest number of different species anywhere (Amazon, Congo, Southeast Asia).
  • Temperate forest — four seasons, rain spread through the year. Oak, beech, sycamore. This is what most of Europe and the eastern USA would look like without human interference. Our UK biome.
  • Coniferous forest (also called taiga). Huge forests of pine, spruce and fir. Cold, snowy winters, short cool summers. Canada, Scandinavia, Russia.
  • Savanna (tropical grassland). Warm year-round but with a dry season. Grasses with scattered trees. African savannas are home to lions, elephants, zebras.
  • Hot desert — very dry (less than 25 cm of rain a year), baking hot days, cold nights. Sahara, Gobi, Arabian desert.
  • Tundra — very cold, with permanently frozen ground called permafrost. Few plants, no trees. Northern Canada, Alaska, Siberia.
  • Polar — ice caps at the very top and bottom of the world. Arctic in the north, Antarctica in the south.

The main thing that decides a place’s biome is its climate — which is decided by how close it is to the Equator (hotter) or the Poles (colder), and how close to the sea.

Topic 4

Trade & Natural Resources

What countries buy and sell to each other.

Trade is simply buying and selling. It happens between people (you trade your pocket money for a bag of sweets), between businesses (shops buy from suppliers), and between countries (the UK buys French cheese; France buys British cars).

Countries trade because no single country has everything it needs. Iceland doesn’t grow pineapples. Saudi Arabia can’t grow much at all but has huge oil reserves. Trade lets both countries get what they need.

What gets traded is usually a natural resource — something that comes from the Earth. Natural resources split into two groups:

  • Renewable resources will never run out, or will keep replacing themselves: the Sun (solar power), wind, water (hydropower and tides), trees (if they’re replanted faster than they’re cut).
  • Non-renewable resources will run out eventually because they’re used up faster than they can form. Coal, oil, natural gas, and metals like iron, gold and copper. Oil took millions of years to form from ancient plankton — we’re burning through it in a couple of centuries.

Two useful words:

  • An import is something a country BUYS from abroad. The UK imports a lot of bananas because they can’t grow here.
  • An export is something a country SELLS abroad. The UK exports cars, Scotch whisky, and financial services.

Countries try to balance the two. If they import a lot more than they export, they can end up owing money.

Topic 5

The Water Cycle

The same cycle, but with more detail.

Year 3 introduced the water cycle in four steps. Year 5 adds some detail and extra names you should know.

  1. Evaporation. The Sun heats water in seas, lakes, rivers and even puddles. Water turns into invisible water vapour and rises into the sky.
  2. Transpiration. This is a new word. Plants draw water up through their roots and release it from their leaves as vapour. Rainforests, with all those millions of plants, release HUGE amounts of water vapour this way.
  3. Condensation. High in the atmosphere, the air is cold. The vapour cools and turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. Billions of those tiny drops gathered together form a cloud.
  4. Precipitation. When the drops in a cloud grow heavy enough, they fall. In warm places they fall as rain. In colder places they fall as snow, sleet, or hail.
  5. Run-off & groundwater. Fallen water doesn’t just lie there. Some runs across the land into streams, then rivers, then out to sea. Some soaks into the soil and becomes groundwater, deep underground. Groundwater eventually flows out into rivers and the sea too.
  6. Collection. All rivers eventually reach the sea. The sea is the ultimate reservoir. From there, evaporation starts everything off again.

This cycle has been going non-stop for billions of years. The water in your drinking glass today was, long ago, a puddle, a cloud, a glacier, and maybe the tear of a Tyrannosaurus.
