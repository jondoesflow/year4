Topic 1

Bigger Numbers & BIDMAS

Up to 10 million, plus the rules for the order of operations.

Year 6 is the final year of primary maths, and numbers get seriously big. We work comfortably with numbers up to 10,000,000 (ten million). That’s roughly the population of London, or the number of grains of rice in 100 kg.

We also use negative numbers in real contexts, not just as a novelty: temperatures below zero, money owed, timezones, golf scores. Working with negatives means you have to think carefully about direction. Adding a negative is like subtracting. Subtracting a negative is like adding. −3 − (−5) = −3 + 5 = 2.

Now, a big Year 6 idea: BIDMAS (also called BODMAS or PEMDAS in other countries). It’s the rulebook for the order of operations — which bit of a sum to do first when there are several things going on.

  • Brackets — anything inside brackets, first.
  • Indices — powers and square roots next.
  • Divide and Multiply — together, working left to right.
  • Add and Subtract — together, working left to right.

Worked example: 3 + 4 × 2. Without BIDMAS you might read left-to-right and get 3 + 4 = 7, then 7 × 2 = 14. Wrong! BIDMAS says multiply first: 4 × 2 = 8, then add: 3 + 8 = 11. Brackets force a different order: (3 + 4) × 2 would be 7 × 2 = 14. Calculators do BIDMAS automatically, but you need to know it to understand what they’re doing.

Topic 2

Long Multiplication & Division

The grown-up methods for tricky numbers.

Some sums are too big to do in your head, even with clever tricks. That’s where the long methods come in. Once you’ve got them, you can multiply and divide absolutely any numbers.

Long multiplication. Imagine you need to work out 246 × 37. You can’t do that in your head. Long multiplication splits the job into pieces. First multiply 246 by 7 (the ones): 246 × 7 = 1,722. Then multiply 246 by 30 (the tens): 246 × 30 = 7,380. Add the two results: 1,722 + 7,380 = 9,102. Done.

You write it out in columns, like this:

  246
×  37
-----
 1722   (246 × 7)
 7380   (246 × 30)
-----
 9102

Long division (often called the bus-stop method). For a sum like 864 ÷ 6, you work from left to right, dividing each digit of the big number in turn, and carrying any remainder over. 8 ÷ 6 = 1 remainder 2, so write 1 and carry the 2. Join the 2 with the next digit (6) to make 26. 26 ÷ 6 = 4 remainder 2. Carry the 2 again to join with the final 4 to make 24. 24 ÷ 6 = 4. Answer: 144.

One golden rule: always estimate first. 864 ÷ 6 is roughly 900 ÷ 6 = 150, so your answer should be around 150. If you ended up with 24 or 1,440, you’d know something had gone wrong.

Topic 3

Algebra

Using letters to stand for numbers.

Until now, every maths problem has used numbers you can see. In Year 6 we meet algebra — a kind of maths where some numbers are hidden behind letters, and your job is to work out what they are. It sounds mysterious but it’s actually just a neat way to write things down.

Simple example. If I say “I’m thinking of a number, I add 5 to it, and I get 12” — what’s my number? You can probably work it out: 7. In algebra, we write that problem as:

x + 5 = 12

To solve the equation, we do the same thing to both sides to get x on its own. Take 5 from both sides: x = 7.

Year 6 algebra covers:

  • Formulas. We can write general rules with letters. The perimeter of a square is 4s where s is the side length. If s = 6 cm, perimeter = 4 × 6 = 24 cm. Same formula, any side length.
  • Solving simple equations. 2n = 10 means “2 times something is 10” — n = 5. Divide both sides by 2.
  • Number sequences. If a sequence goes 3, 7, 11, 15, 19… we can describe the rule (add 4 each time) and even work out the 100th term.

Once you can use letters confidently, a whole new kind of maths opens up — it’s the door to secondary school maths.

Topic 4

Fractions, Decimals, Percentages, Ratio

All the ways to share amounts.

Year 6 uses all four operations on fractions. Here’s how each one works:

  • Adding and subtracting. Only works if the fractions have the same denominator (bottom number). 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. If denominators differ, first convert them to match: 1/2 + 1/3 needs a common denominator of 6, so that’s 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.
  • Multiplying fractions. Easy — multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms. 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6. 2/3 × 4/5 = 8/15.
  • Dividing fractions. Slightly weird. You flip the second fraction and then multiply. 1/2 ÷ 1/4 is the same as 1/2 × 4/1 = 4/2 = 2. Think of it like this: how many quarters fit into a half? Two.

Percentages of numbers. 20% of 150 = 0.20 × 150 = 30. Quick tricks: 10% is dividing by 10; 1% is dividing by 100; 50% is half; 25% is a quarter.

Ratio is a new idea: comparing two amounts. Writing 2 : 3 means “for every 2 of the first thing, there are 3 of the second”. If you mix paint in the ratio 2 : 3 (red : blue), and you use 10 parts in total, 4 of them are red and 6 are blue.

To split something in a ratio, add the parts to find the total units, then divide and multiply. Split 20 in the ratio 2 : 3: total units = 5. One unit = 20 ÷ 5 = 4. So you get 2 × 4 = 8 and 3 × 4 = 12.

Proportion is similar: “3 out of every 5 people” is a proportion. It’s essentially a ratio expressed as part-to-whole.

Topic 5

Geometry & Coordinates

Shapes, angles and pinpointing locations.

Year 6 geometry really sharpens up. You’ll be expected to know these facts off by heart:

  • Angles on a straight line add up to 180°.
  • Angles around a point add up to 360° (a full turn).
  • Angles in any triangle always add up to 180°. Always, no matter the shape of the triangle.
  • Angles in any quadrilateral (four-sided shape) always add up to 360°.

These rules let you work out missing angles. If a triangle has two angles of 50° and 60°, the third must be 180 − 50 − 60 = 70°.

Coordinates in four quadrants is a big step up from before. Until now, coordinates have only used positive numbers. In Year 6, the grid extends LEFT of zero (negative x) and BELOW zero (negative y) as well. Every point is written as (x, y) — always x first.

The four quadrants are named after the four parts of the grid, going anti-clockwise:

  • Top right: both x and y positive.
  • Top left: x negative, y positive.
  • Bottom left: both negative.
  • Bottom right: x positive, y negative.

Useful shape vocabulary:

  • A polygon is any flat shape with straight sides.
  • Regular polygon means all sides equal, all angles equal (e.g. a regular hexagon).
  • Irregular polygon means the sides or angles differ.
  • 3D shapes have faces (flat sides), edges (where two faces meet), and vertices (corners). A cube has 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices.
Topic 6

Statistics

Pie charts, line graphs and the mean average.

Year 6 statistics adds two important new ideas: pie charts and the mean average.

Pie charts are circles split into slices (like a pie). Each slice’s size shows a proportion of the total. The whole circle is 100% (or 360°). If 25% of a class has blue eyes, the blue-eye slice takes up a quarter of the pie. Pie charts are great for showing “how this total splits up”, but less good for precise numbers.

Line graphs (useful for change over time) and bar charts (useful for comparisons) should both feel familiar now.

The mean average is the most important average. To find the mean, add all the numbers and then divide by how many there are.

Example: find the mean of 2, 4, 6, 8. Total = 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 = 20. How many numbers? 4. Mean = 20 ÷ 4 = 5. The mean is like a fair share — if these numbers were lengths of sweet, and 4 kids were splitting them fairly, each would get 5.

When is the mean useful? Anywhere you want a typical value:

  • Mean temperature for July tells you how warm July usually is.
  • Mean test score tells you how well the class did overall.
  • Mean time to finish a race tells you the typical runner’s speed.

Watch out: extreme values can distort the mean. If 4 friends spend £2, £2, ¢3 and £93 at the shop, the mean spend is £25 — but that doesn’t describe any of them well. Statistics is always a bit of art as well as science.
