Topic 1

Place Value

Every digit has a home. Knowing its home tells you how big it is.

In Year 4 we work with numbers up to 10,000. Each digit sits in a column that tells us its value:

  • Thousands — 1,000s column
  • Hundreds — 100s column
  • Tens — 10s column
  • Ones — 1s column

So in 3,472, the 3 means three thousands, the 4 means four hundreds, the 7 means seven tens, and the 2 means two ones.

We also learn Roman numerals from I to C. You see them on clocks and old buildings.

Roman numerals
  • I = 1
  • V = 5
  • X = 10
  • L = 50
  • C = 100
  • IV = 4 · IX = 9 · XL = 40
Negative numbers

Numbers below zero. On a cold day the thermometer might read −3°C — three degrees below freezing.

Topic 2

Addition & Subtraction

Join groups together. Or take some away.

In Year 4 we add and subtract 4-digit numbers using the column method. Line up the columns, then add or subtract from the right (ones first).

When a column’s answer is 10 or more, we carry the extra over to the next column. When we can’t take a digit away from a smaller one, we borrow from the next column.

Always check your answer by doing the opposite operation. If 3,215 + 1,478 = 4,693, then 4,693 − 1,478 should give 3,215.

Column method 
  3,215
+ 1,478
-------
  4,693

Carry the 1 from 5+8=13 into the tens column.

Topic 3

Times Tables

By the end of Year 4 you should know all times tables up to 12 × 12.

Multiplication is just repeated adding. 4 × 3 means “four lots of three”, which is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.

Division is the opposite. 12 ÷ 3 means “how many threes fit into twelve?” The answer is 4.

Top tips for learning tables:

  • The 2, 5 and 10 tables are the quickest to learn.
  • Any number × 1 is itself. Any number × 0 is zero.
  • 3 × 4 is the same as 4 × 3 — it doesn’t matter which way round.
  • The 9 times table has a pattern — the digits add up to 9 every time (9, 18, 27, 36… 1+8=9, 2+7=9).
A 6 times table
  • 1 × 6 = 6
  • 2 × 6 = 12
  • 3 × 6 = 18
  • 4 × 6 = 24
  • 5 × 6 = 30
  • 6 × 6 = 36
  • 7 × 6 = 42
  • 8 × 6 = 48
  • 9 × 6 = 54
  • 10 × 6 = 60
  • 11 × 6 = 66
  • 12 × 6 = 72
Topic 4

Fractions & Decimals

A fraction is a slice of a whole thing.

In the fraction 3/4, the bottom number (4) is the denominator — it says how many slices the whole is cut into. The top number (3) is the numerator — it says how many slices we have.

Equivalent fractions are fractions that look different but are really the same. 1/2 is the same as 2/4, which is the same as 4/8.

Decimals are another way to write fractions where the denominator is 10 or 100.

  • 0.1 means 1 tenth (1/10)
  • 0.25 means 25 hundredths (25/100 or 1/4)
  • 0.5 means 5 tenths (1/2)
Equivalent fractions

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 = 5/10 = 0.5

1/4 = 2/8 = 25/100 = 0.25

3/4 = 6/8 = 75/100 = 0.75

Topic 5

Measurement

Time, money, length, and the space around a shape.

Time. Read an analogue clock (the one with hands) and a 12-hour or 24-hour digital clock. Work out how long it is from one time to another.

Money. Add and subtract pounds and pence. 100 pence = £1.

Length. 100 cm = 1 m. 1,000 m = 1 km. 1,000 ml = 1 litre. 1,000 g = 1 kg.

Perimeter is the distance all the way around the outside of a shape. For a rectangle, perimeter = (length + width) × 2.

Area is the space inside a shape. For a rectangle, area = length × width.

Quick conversions
  • 100 cm = 1 m
  • 1,000 m = 1 km
  • 1,000 ml = 1 litre
  • 1,000 g = 1 kg
  • 60 seconds = 1 minute
  • 60 minutes = 1 hour
  • 24 hours = 1 day
Topic 6

Geometry

The maths of shapes, angles, and symmetry.

2D shapes are flat. 3D shapes have depth. In Year 4 you need to recognise and describe:

  • Triangles — equilateral, isosceles, scalene, right-angled.
  • Quadrilaterals — square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, trapezium.
  • Regular polygons — pentagon (5), hexagon (6), heptagon (7), octagon (8).

A shape has a line of symmetry if one half is a mirror image of the other. A square has 4 lines of symmetry. A circle has an infinite number!

Coordinates tell you a point’s position. We write them as (x, y) — how far across, then how far up. Always x before y.

Topic 7

Statistics

Picking information out of a chart or graph.

In Year 4 we read and draw:

  • Bar charts — each bar shows a quantity. The taller the bar, the bigger the number.
  • Pictograms — small pictures, each standing for a number (for example, one cat picture = 2 cats).
  • Line graphs — dots joined by lines. They show how something changes over time, like temperature over a week.

When you read a graph, always check the key or the scale first. A scale of “2 per square” means each square on the y-axis is worth 2, not 1.
