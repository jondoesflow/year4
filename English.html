Topic 1

Reading Comprehension

Understanding what a story or article is really saying.

Good readers do more than decode the words. They picture the scene, ask questions, and notice how the characters feel.

When you read something new, try these steps:

  • Predict — before you read, guess what might happen from the title and pictures.
  • Clarify — if a word is tricky, read the sentence around it. The surrounding words often give the meaning.
  • Question — ask yourself why a character did what they did.
  • Summarise — in your own words, say what the paragraph is about.

Inference means working out something that the writer hasn’t said directly. If a character slams a door, the writer hasn’t said “she is angry” — but you can infer it.

Top reading tip

If a sentence doesn’t make sense, read it again slowly. Then read the sentence BEFORE it. Context almost always explains tricky words.

Topic 2

Grammar

The rules that tell us how words fit together.

Nouns are people, places or things. Verbs are actions (run, laugh, think). Adjectives describe nouns (a tall tree). Adverbs describe verbs (she ran quickly).

A fronted adverbial is a word or phrase at the start of a sentence that tells us when, where, or how something happens:

  • Yesterday, we went to the park. (when)
  • In the corner, a cat was sleeping. (where)
  • Very carefully, she picked up the egg. (how)

Always follow a fronted adverbial with a comma.

An expanded noun phrase is a noun with extra describing words: “the dog” becomes “the big, spotty dog with the wagging tail.”

Word class cheat sheet
  • Noun — a thing (chair, river, Emma)
  • Verb — an action (jump, eat, think)
  • Adjective — describes a noun (red, loud, tiny)
  • Adverb — describes a verb (quickly, silently)
  • Pronoun — replaces a noun (he, she, it, they)
  • Conjunction — joins ideas (and, but, because, although)
Topic 3

Punctuation

The little marks that make writing easy to read.

An apostrophe does two jobs:

  • Shortening (also called a contraction): do not → don’t, I am → I’m, she will → she’ll.
  • Possession (showing it belongs to someone): Tom’s ball, the dog’s bowl.

Watch out! Its (belonging to it) has no apostrophe. It’s with an apostrophe always means “it is”.

Direct speech — when you write what someone says, use inverted commas (“speech marks”) around the words:

  • “I’m hungry,” said Liam.
  • Mum smiled and said, “Dinner is ready!”

Every new speaker starts on a new line.

Direct speech checklist
  1. Open inverted commas before the first spoken word.
  2. Start the speech with a capital letter.
  3. End with a comma, full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark BEFORE the closing inverted commas.
  4. Close inverted commas after the last spoken word.
  5. A new speaker starts on a new line.
Topic 4

Spelling

Patterns and tricky words to watch for.

A prefix goes at the start of a word. It changes the meaning:

  • un- means “not” → unhappy, unkind, unfair.
  • dis- means “not” or “opposite of” → disagree, disappear.
  • re- means “again” → redo, rewrite, return.
  • pre- means “before” → preheat, preview, prepay.

A suffix goes at the end:

  • -ly turns an adjective into an adverb: quick → quickly.
  • -ful means “full of”: care → careful, help → helpful.
  • -less means “without”: care → careless, hope → hopeless.

Homophones sound the same but mean different things: there / their / they’re, to / too / two, your / you’re, here / hear.

Topic 5

Writing

Turning your ideas into stories, letters, reports and poems.

All good writers do four things:

  • Plan — quickly jot down what will happen, in order.
  • Draft — write the first version. Don’t worry about mistakes yet.
  • Edit — read it back. Fix spelling, punctuation, and swap in stronger words.
  • Publish — write it out neatly, or type it up.

Try to show, don’t tell. Instead of “She was sad”, write “Her eyes filled with tears and her shoulders slumped.” The reader pictures the feeling themselves.

Vary your sentence lengths. A mixture of short and long sentences keeps writing interesting.

Story-starter ideas
  • The door at the end of the attic wouldn’t open. Until today…
  • A single footprint in the fresh snow led into the woods.
  • She opened the old book and a tiny voice said her name.
  • The river suddenly stopped flowing. Nobody knew why.
Topic 6

Vocabulary

The more words you know, the more colours you can paint with.

A synonym is a word that means the same as another word. Instead of writing “big” every time, you could use: huge, enormous, massive, gigantic, vast.

An antonym is a word that means the opposite. The antonym of “big” is “small” or “tiny”.

When you write, try to swap tired words for sharper ones:

  • “said” → whispered, shouted, mumbled, declared, replied
  • “went” → trudged, skipped, raced, strolled, marched
  • “nice” → kind, friendly, thoughtful, gentle, warm
A thesaurus is your friend

A thesaurus is a book (or website) that gives you synonyms for any word. Keep one nearby when you edit!
