Topic 1

UK Mountains & Rivers

The shape of our country.

If you looked at the UK from space, you’d see a country that’s surprisingly wrinkled. All those mountains, hills, rivers and valleys are called physical features — the bits that nature made. In Year 3 we learn the big ones.

Most of the UK’s mountains are in the north and west. The south and east are gentler — rolling hills and flat plains. Mountains to know:

  • Ben Nevis in Scotland — tallest in the UK at 1,345 m. In Scottish Gaelic, Ben means mountain.
  • Snowdon in North Wales — tallest in Wales at 1,085 m. A popular one to climb.
  • Scafell Pike in the Lake District — tallest in England.
  • Slieve Donard — tallest in Northern Ireland.

The UK’s rivers are just as important as its mountains. Rivers carved the valleys, gave us our cities, and powered our industries.

  • River Severn — the longest in the UK at 220 miles. It rises in Wales and flows into the Bristol Channel.
  • River Thames — flows through London. Second-longest at 215 miles.
  • River Trent — flows through the Midlands and into the Humber.

If you want to dig deeper into rivers specifically, there’s a whole guidebook on this site called Rivers that goes through the full journey from source to sea.

Topic 2

Countries of Europe

Our big continent.

Europe is the continent the UK sits in. It’s the second-smallest of the seven continents, but it’s packed with different countries — around 44 in total, from tiny Vatican City (the smallest country in the world) to enormous Russia (the biggest country in the world, stretching halfway across Asia too).

Here are some major European countries and their capital cities:

  • France — Paris. Home of the Eiffel Tower.
  • Spain — Madrid. Famous for flamenco dancing and paella.
  • Italy — Rome. Where pizza and pasta were invented.
  • Germany — Berlin. Europe’s biggest country by population.
  • Netherlands — Amsterdam. Famous for tulips, windmills and canals.
  • Portugal — Lisbon. On the west coast, facing the Atlantic.
  • Greece — Athens. Home of the Ancient Olympics.
  • Ireland — Dublin. The UK’s closest neighbour across the sea.

Europe has two huge mountain ranges: the Alps, running across the middle-south of the continent (Switzerland, Austria, France, Italy), and the Pyrenees, separating France from Spain. Big rivers include the Rhine, the Danube and the Volga (the longest in Europe, in Russia).

Topic 3

Settlements

Where people choose to live.

A settlement is a place where people live together. It can be as small as a handful of houses or as huge as a city of millions. When you look at a map, every dot represents people who once chose to settle there — and they chose for good reasons.

Early settlers (thousands of years ago) looked for places with:

  • Water — a river, stream or spring nearby. You can’t survive long without water to drink, wash and water crops.
  • Flat land — good for building houses, planting fields and keeping animals.
  • Shelter — protection from wind, rain, and enemies. Valleys and forests gave cover.
  • Food sources — rich soil for farming, forests with animals to hunt, rivers with fish.
  • Good routes — roads, river crossings, or sea access, so they could trade with other places.

London was founded by the Romans on the River Thames because it was a handy crossing point AND far enough inland to be defended AND accessible by sea. Most old towns have a similar story — look at a map of where cities sit, and you’ll often spot a river.

Settlements come in sizes, and they have different names:

  • Hamlet — just a few houses together, smaller than a village.
  • Village — a small community, usually with a church, a pub, a post office.
  • Town — bigger, with shops, schools, and more people.
  • City — really big. In the UK, cities are usually places with a cathedral.
Topic 4

The Water Cycle

Water goes round and round the Earth.

Here’s a mind-bending fact: the water you drink today has been on Earth for billions of years. Dinosaurs drank this water. Roman soldiers bathed in it. It’s been round the loop so many times, and none of it is ever really lost — it just changes form and keeps moving.

The journey water takes, again and again, is called the water cycle. It has four main steps:

  1. Evaporation. The Sun heats up water in seas, lakes, rivers and puddles. The warmth makes tiny bits of water turn into an invisible gas called water vapour. The vapour rises up into the sky.
  2. Condensation. High up in the atmosphere, the air is cold. The water vapour cools down and turns back into lots of tiny water droplets. When millions of droplets gather together, you get a cloud.
  3. Precipitation. When the droplets in a cloud get heavy enough, they fall out of the sky. If it’s warm, they fall as rain. If the air is freezing, they fall as snow or hail. This step is called precipitation (remember it — it’s the fancy word for “stuff falling from clouds”).
  4. Collection. The fallen water joins streams and rivers, and eventually flows back to the sea. Some of it sinks into the soil and becomes groundwater. Then the Sun heats it again, and… off we go.

The cycle never stops. Not during the day, not at night, not in summer, not in winter. Water moves around our planet constantly.

Topic 5

How We Use the Land

Every piece of land does a job.

If you look carefully at any landscape — from an aeroplane, from a hill, or just along your street — you’ll notice that almost every piece of land has a job. Geographers group these jobs into categories:

  • Residential land is where people live. Houses, blocks of flats, estates.
  • Commercial land is for business. Shops, supermarkets, offices, restaurants, cafes.
  • Industrial land is where things get made or stored. Factories, warehouses, big car parks for lorries.
  • Agricultural land is for farming. Fields of crops, pastures for cattle, orchards.
  • Leisure land is for fun. Parks, sports fields, playgrounds, golf courses, football stadiums.
  • Transport land moves people and goods around. Roads, railways, canals, airports, ports.

A town might have a town centre (mostly commercial), ringed by streets of houses (residential), with schools and parks dotted between (leisure), a retail park on the edge (commercial), an industrial estate further out (industrial), and farms beyond that (agricultural). It’s like a giant map quilt.

Land use changes over time. A field might become houses. An old factory might become flats. When you walk around your local area, try spotting the categories — it’s a geographer’s game.
