Topic 1

Active & Passive Voice

Who is doing the action, and who it’s done to.

Every English sentence has a subject (the doer) and usually an object (the thing having stuff done to it). In active voice, the subject comes first and the action flows from subject to object. It’s the normal way we talk and write.

The cat (subject) chased the mouse (object).

In passive voice, the sentence flips. The object moves to the front. The verb changes form to “was/were + past participle”. Sometimes the subject is mentioned afterwards with “by”, and sometimes it’s dropped altogether.

The mouse was chased (by the cat).

Same information, different emphasis. Passive voice is useful when:

  • The action matters more than the doer: “The window was broken” (maybe nobody knows who did it).
  • You don’t want to name the doer: “Mistakes were made.” (A classic politician’s line.)
  • You want a formal, detached tone: scientific and news writing use passive a lot.

Watch out: overusing passive makes writing dull and wordy. Active usually has more life. Writers switch between the two deliberately — that’s a Year 6 level skill.

Topic 2

Subjunctive Form

Polite, formal phrasing.

The subjunctive is a slightly unusual form of the verb. It pops up in formal requests, suggestions, and wishes. You probably already use it without realising — the famous song “If I were a rich man…” is in the subjunctive. Not “if I WAS” (which is grammatically wrong, even though loads of people say it); proper English says “if I WERE”.

Two patterns to recognise:

  • “Were” instead of “was” in hypotheticals. “If I were a rich person…” “If I were you, I’d apologise.” “I wish I were taller.”
  • Bare verb (infinitive without “to”) after suggest/insist/demand. “I suggest that he be on time.” (Not “is”.) “It is important that she take her medicine.” (Not “takes”.) “We insist that he attend.”

Subjunctive feels slightly old-fashioned and very formal. You wouldn’t use it chatting with friends. It’s for polite emails, formal writing, and for knowing it’s there when you read it in other people’s writing.

Topic 3

Colons, Semicolons & Bullets

Powerful punctuation for upper-year writing.

Year 6 punctuation adds two heavyweights: the colon and the semicolon. They look similar but do different jobs, and they’re sometimes used by adults incorrectly. You can get them right.

A colon : signals “here it comes”. Use it to introduce something:

  • A list: “You will need: flour, eggs, sugar, butter.”
  • An explanation: “He loved one thing above all: football.”
  • A quote: “She said just one thing: ‘I told you so.’”

A semicolon ; joins two complete sentences that are closely related. You could use a full stop instead, but the semicolon shows the two are linked.

The dog barked; the cat scurried away.

Rule: what sits either side of a semicolon MUST be able to stand as a full sentence on its own. If either side is just a fragment, you’ve used a comma or a colon by mistake.

Bullet points are another Year 6 skill. They help organise a list. Rules:

  • Be consistent. If one bullet starts with a capital and ends with a full stop, they all should.
  • Don’t mix complete sentences with fragments in the same list.
  • Usually introduce the list with a colon.
Topic 4

Formal & Informal (Year 6)

Reading the room.

By Year 6, you can spot the difference between formal and informal writing instantly. You also know how to WRITE in either register depending on the situation.

Clues that writing is formal:

  • Uses full words, not contractions. “Do not” instead of “don’t”. “Would not” instead of “wouldn’t”.
  • Uses passive voice more often: “The form should be submitted” not “Send us the form”.
  • Uses subjunctive when needed: “We require that she attend”.
  • Uses sophisticated, precise vocabulary: request, require, approximately, furthermore, however, nonetheless, significant, considerable, therefore.
  • Avoids slang, chatty phrases, and exclamation marks.
  • Often uses a polite third-person structure: “It is requested that…” instead of “Please will you…”.

Clues that writing is informal:

  • Contractions everywhere: don’t, can’t, won’t, it’s.
  • Chatty phrases: “kinda”, “loads of”, “anyway”.
  • Exclamation marks, emojis, ha-has.
  • First person, personal voice: “I can’t believe…” “I reckon…”

Use formal for: letters of complaint, job applications, newspaper articles, essays, scientific reports, emails to people you don’t know.

Use informal for: texts to friends, diaries, emails to your mum, stories (often), social media (usually).

Mixing the two within one piece of writing looks sloppy. Decide your register at the start and stick to it.

Topic 5

Grammar Recap

All the important grammar terms.

Year 6 pulls together everything you’ve learned about grammar since Year 1. Here’s a whistle-stop tour.

Word classes — the jobs words do:

  • Noun — a thing (cat, London, courage).
  • Verb — an action or state (run, is, think).
  • Adjective — describes a noun (red, loud, small).
  • Adverb — describes a verb, adjective, or adverb (quickly, very, tomorrow).
  • Pronoun — replaces a noun (he, she, it, they, who).
  • Preposition — position or direction (under, through, with, before).
  • Conjunction — joins ideas (and, but, because, although).
  • Determiner — introduces a noun (the, a, an, this, my, some, several).

Sentence types:

  • Simple — one main clause. I ran.
  • Compound — two main clauses joined by and/but/or. I ran and I laughed.
  • Complex — a main clause plus one or more subordinate clauses. I ran, although I was tired.

Subordinate clauses often start with: although, because, if, since, unless, while, when, as.

Tenses:

  • Past simple: I ran.
  • Present simple: I run.
  • Future: I will run.
  • Present perfect: I have run (it happened and is still relevant).
  • Past perfect: I had run (it happened before another past thing).
  • Present continuous: I am running (happening right now).
Topic 6

Year 6 Tricky Words

The spelling curriculum for Y6.

The Year 6 statutory spelling list. Every Year 6 pupil should be able to spell these. Most adults can’t spell them all — so don’t feel bad if some take time.

accommodate, accompany, according, achieve, aggressive, amateur, ancient, apparent, appreciate, attached, available, average, awkward, bargain, bruise, category, cemetery, committee, communicate, community, competition, conscience, conscious, controversy, convenience, correspond, criticise, curiosity, definite, desperate, determined, develop, dictionary, disastrous, embarrass, environment, equip, especially, exaggerate, excellent, existence, explanation, familiar, foreign, forty, frequently, government, guarantee, harass, hindrance, identity, immediately, individual, interfere, interrupt, language, leisure, lightning, marvellous, mischievous, muscle, necessary, neighbour, nuisance, occupy, occur, opportunity, parliament, persuade, physical, prejudice, privilege, profession, programme, pronunciation, queue, recognise, recommend, relevant, restaurant, rhyme, rhythm, sacrifice, secretary, shoulder, signature, sincere, soldier, stomach, sufficient, suggest, symbol, system, temperature, thorough, twelfth, variety, vegetable, vehicle, yacht

Patterns that trip people up:

  • Double letters in the middle: accommodate, necessary, embarrass. Learn each one individually.
  • Silent letters: island, rhythm, Parliament.
  • Unusual vowel spellings: bruise, yacht, physical.
  • Tricky endings: -tion, -sion, -cious, -tious. Science tactic: these all sound like “shun”.

Top strategy: Look → Say → Cover → Write → Check. Do this once a day with a handful of words and you’ll have them cracked before the SATs.
