Topic 1

Long Ago vs Today

Life used to look very different!

If you could travel back in time to when your grandparents were little, you’d get a big shock. Lots of things we use every single day just didn’t exist back then. The past isn’t just “the same but older” — it was genuinely different.

Here’s what was missing when your grandparents were your age:

  • No smartphones. No touch screens at all.
  • No internet. If you wanted to know something, you looked in a book at the library.
  • Televisions had only three or four channels, and in the very early days the pictures were in black and white.
  • No email, no text messages. If you wanted to say something to someone far away, you wrote them a letter and posted it.
  • No video games. Kids played outside for hours, rain or shine.

But here’s the lovely thing: a lot of things haven’t changed at all. Skipping ropes, footballs, teddy bears, dolls, puzzles, building bricks — your grandparents played with these too. Some toys have been around for hundreds of years.

Next time you see a grandparent, ask them what they played with when they were 6. You might be surprised.

Topic 2

Transport Through Time

How people got around.

Getting from place to place has changed more in the last 200 years than in the thousands of years before. Your great-great-great-grandparents would be astonished at cars, trains and planes.

Let’s travel through the history of transport:

  • Long, long ago: people walked everywhere. If they had to go far, they rode on horses, or in carts pulled by horses. If they needed to cross water, they used boats with sails or oars.
  • About 200 years ago: the first trains with steam engines chugged across Britain. For the first time, people could travel faster than a galloping horse. It must have felt like magic.
  • About 130 years ago: the first cars appeared. They were noisy, slow, and very expensive. Hardly anyone had one.
  • 1903: two American brothers called Orville and Wilbur Wright (the Wright brothers) built the world’s first aeroplane. The first flight only lasted 12 seconds, but it changed everything.
  • Today: we have super-fast trains, millions of cars, passenger planes crossing the whole world in hours, and even electric scooters.

Each new invention made the world a little bit smaller. Places that used to take weeks to reach now take hours.

Topic 3

Queen Elizabeth II

A queen for 70 years.

For most of the last 70 years, the United Kingdom had the same queen. Her name was Elizabeth II, and she reigned longer than any other British king or queen in history. That’s a really astonishing record.

Elizabeth was born in 1926. She became queen in 1952, when she was only 25 years old. Her father, King George VI, had died suddenly. She was actually away in Kenya at the time, and she found out she was queen while she was up a tree watching wildlife.

She stayed queen for 70 years. Through her reign she met 15 different Prime Ministers, watched the first man walk on the Moon, and saw the internet change the world. She died in September 2022, aged 96. Many, many people lined the streets of London to say goodbye.

Her son Charles became the new king — King Charles III. One day his eldest son, Prince William, will be king. This long line of kings and queens passing down through one family is called a monarchy.

Topic 4

Mary Anning

A girl who found dinosaur bones.

Mary Anning was born in 1799, in a little seaside town called Lyme Regis on the south coast of England. Her family was poor. Her dad sold fossils to tourists to make some extra money, and he taught Mary how to find them on the beach.

When Mary was just 12 years old, she and her brother found something incredible sticking out of a cliff. It was the first complete skeleton of an ichthyosaur — a giant sea reptile that lived 200 million years ago. Imagine: a 12-year-old discovering a creature nobody had ever seen before!

Mary kept finding more amazing fossils all her life. She found the first complete plesiosaur (another sea reptile) and the first British pterosaur (a flying reptile). Her discoveries helped scientists work out that dinosaurs and giant reptiles really had walked the Earth long, long before humans.

Because Mary was a woman and poor, she didn’t get the credit she deserved at the time. The rich men who bought her fossils put them in museums and often forgot to mention her. Today, she is finally recognised as one of the most important fossil hunters who ever lived.

Topic 5

Brave Explorers

People who went where no one had gone before.

Throughout history, some people have looked at the edge of what’s known and thought: “What’s out THERE?” Then they’ve packed their bags and gone to find out. We call these people explorers.

In 1492, an Italian sailor called Christopher Columbus sailed west across the huge Atlantic Ocean in three small wooden ships. He hoped to find a new route to Asia. Instead, he accidentally found the continent we now call the Americas. It was a journey that changed the world.

Jump forward to 1932. An American pilot called Amelia Earhart became the first woman ever to fly a plane ALONE across the Atlantic Ocean. She flew through storms and icy skies for nearly 15 hours. She proved that women could do anything men could do.

On 20 July 1969, an American astronaut called Neil Armstrong took the most famous walk in history — a walk ON THE MOON. He rode a huge rocket called Apollo 11 all the way there, and when he stepped out he said: “That’s one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.” Humans had never before stood on another world.

Each of these people did something nobody else had ever done. Next time you feel nervous about trying something new, think of them.
