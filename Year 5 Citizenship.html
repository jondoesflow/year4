Topic 1

Children’s Rights Worldwide

The UN Convention.

In 1989, almost every country in the world signed an agreement called the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. It promises every child:

  • The right to a name and a nationality.
  • The right to be safe from harm and abuse.
  • The right to good food and a home.
  • The right to education.
  • The right to play and rest.
  • The right to medical care.
  • The right to be listened to.
  • The right to grow up in their own family if possible.

UNICEF is the part of the United Nations that looks after children’s rights all over the world. They send food, medicine, vaccines, and teachers to places where children need them most.

Topic 2

Fair Trade

Helping farmers worldwide.

A lot of food in your kitchen — chocolate, bananas, coffee, tea, sugar — was grown by farmers in poor countries. Big companies sometimes pay them very little. The farmers stay poor while the companies get rich.

Fairtrade is a movement that pays farmers a fair price. Look on the back of food packets in the supermarket: if it has the green and blue Fairtrade logo, the farmers got paid properly.

Year 5 thinks about choices like:

  • Buying Fairtrade when you can.
  • Knowing where food comes from.
  • Asking schools to use Fairtrade products.
Topic 3

Charities

Helping people in need.

A charity is a group that exists to help others. They’re funded by donations from ordinary people, not by the government.

Some famous UK charities:

  • British Red Cross — helps people in crisis.
  • Cancer Research UK — funds cancer research.
  • Save the Children — helps children worldwide.
  • Macmillan Cancer Support — supports cancer patients and families.
  • RSPCA — looks after mistreated animals.
  • Children in Need — BBC charity that funds children’s projects.

Even small amounts of money help. Schools often raise funds with bake sales, sponsored runs, or non-uniform days.

Topic 4

News & Media

Telling fact from opinion.

News tells us what’s happening in the world. We get news from TV (BBC News, ITV), websites (BBC News, Guardian), newspapers, and social media.

Not everything you read is true. Important skills:

  • Check more than one source. If only one website says something, be cautious.
  • Spot facts vs opinions. “It rained in London today” is a fact. “The rain was awful” is an opinion.
  • Beware of headlines designed to shock. Sometimes they exaggerate.
  • Photos and videos can be edited. AI can now make completely fake ones.

Asking “Who wrote this and why?” is a powerful question.

Topic 5

Global Citizens

Belonging to one world.

You belong to your family, your school, your town, your country. You also belong to one bigger group: the whole world. We’re all global citizens.

Big problems don’t stop at borders:

  • Climate change affects everyone.
  • Diseases (like covid in 2020) travel between countries.
  • Pollution in one country drifts into the next.
  • Wars in one country send refugees to others.

Solving these problems means countries working together. The United Nations (UN) was set up in 1945 after World War II so that countries could talk and try to keep the peace. Today 193 countries are members.
