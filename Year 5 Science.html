Topic 1

Life Cycles

How living things grow and reproduce.

Every living thing on Earth, from a bacterium too small to see to the biggest blue whale, goes through a life cycle. It’s the sequence of stages from birth to reproduction to old age to death. The details differ wildly between creatures, but every life cycle involves reproducing — making the next generation — at some point. That’s how life keeps going.

Let’s look at the main groups:

  • Mammals (including humans, dogs, whales, elephants). A baby is born live from its mother, drinks milk from her, grows into a child, then an adult, and eventually gets old. Along the way, adult mammals might have their own babies.
  • Birds. Parents build a nest, lay eggs, and sit on them to keep them warm. Chicks hatch out and are fed by their parents until they can fly. Once they’re grown, they find a mate and lay eggs of their own.
  • Amphibians, like frogs. Eggs are laid in water. They hatch into wriggly tadpoles that live in water and breathe with gills. Slowly, a tadpole grows legs, loses its tail, grows lungs, and hops out as a frog. Water → land.
  • Insects, like butterflies. A tiny egg hatches into a larva (a caterpillar), which eats leaves like crazy and grows. It then forms a protective pupa (a chrysalis), and inside this it completely rearranges itself into an adult butterfly. This transformation is called metamorphosis.
  • Plants. A seed sprouts and grows into a seedling, then a mature plant with leaves. The plant flowers. A bee pollinates the flower. The flower turns into fruit with seeds inside. The seeds fall (or get spread by animals), and new plants grow.

Notice the common thread: each stage has its own job, and each generation prepares the way for the next.

Topic 2

Humans: Growing Up

The stages of a human life.

A human life stretches from a tiny baby to an elderly grown-up, often over 80 or 90 years. Year 5 introduces the names for the main stages we pass through:

  1. Baby (roughly 0 to 2 years). Completely dependent on adults. Learning everything from scratch.
  2. Child (roughly 2 to 11). Growing, learning to read and write, going to school, making friends. This is where you are now.
  3. Adolescent (roughly 11 to 19). The tricky in-between stage. Sometimes called a teenager. Growing very fast, physically and emotionally.
  4. Adult (roughly 20 to 65). Fully grown. Working, often having their own children, running their own life.
  5. Old age (65 and up). Slowing down. Often retiring from work. Grandparents are usually in this stage.

During adolescence, the body changes a lot — this is called puberty. Puberty prepares the body to be an adult and eventually (if the person chooses) to have children of their own. Different bodies go through puberty at different times — anywhere between about 10 and 16 is normal.

Puberty is a gentle topic in Year 5, and a bigger one in Year 6. The main thing to know for now is that it’s a totally normal and natural part of growing up, and everyone goes through it.

Topic 3

Properties of Materials

What makes materials useful, and how they change.

When an engineer is designing a bridge, or a toy, or a kitchen knife, they have to pick the right material for the job. They look at a material’s properties — the features that make it useful or useless for a specific purpose.

Key properties we test:

  • Hardness — can it be scratched or dented easily? Diamond is very hard; chalk is soft.
  • Transparency — can you see through it? Glass is transparent; wood is opaque.
  • Electrical conductivity — does electricity flow through it? Copper wires are conductors; rubber is an insulator (it blocks electricity).
  • Thermal conductivity — does heat flow through it? A metal spoon gets hot fast in soup; a wooden spoon doesn’t.
  • Magnetism — does a magnet stick to it? Iron yes, aluminium no.
  • Solubility — does it dissolve in water? Sugar and salt dissolve; sand doesn’t.

Materials can change form, and there are two big categories of change:

Reversible changes can be undone. Freeze water into ice and you can melt it back. Dissolve salt in water and you can evaporate the water to get the salt back. Mix sand and water and you can filter the sand out.

Irreversible changes are permanent — you can’t undo them. Burn paper and you cannot un-burn it. Bake a cake and you can’t turn it back into raw ingredients. Mix vinegar with bicarbonate of soda (fizzy!) and the fizz makes a brand new substance.

Lots of cooking and chemistry is about knowing which changes are which.

Topic 4

Forces

Gravity, friction, air resistance, water resistance.

A force is just a push or a pull. Year 5 digs into the main forces you meet in the world.

Gravity is the big one. It’s the invisible force pulling everything on Earth towards the centre of the planet. That’s why things fall down and not up, why we stay on the ground and not drift off into space. The story (probably a myth, but a good one) is that Sir Isaac Newton worked out gravity after watching an apple fall from a tree in his garden in 1666. He realised the same force pulling the apple down was keeping the Moon in orbit around the Earth.

Friction is the force that slows things down when they rub against each other. If you push a book across a table, friction is what eventually stops it. Friction is why you can’t keep sliding on the floor forever, and why cars need engines to keep going. Rough surfaces have more friction; smooth or slippery surfaces have less.

Air resistance is friction from AIR. When anything moves through air, the air pushes back against it. Drop a feather and a stone at the same time: the stone falls fast, the feather floats down slowly, because the feather has lots of air resistance for its weight. Parachutes work by having LOTS of air resistance — they slow your fall.

Water resistance is the same idea, but in water. Boats and fish are streamlined (smooth, pointy shapes) to move through water with less resistance. A flat piece of wood is hard to push through water; a canoe glides through easily.

Finally, we meet simple machines: levers, pulleys, and gears. These are clever arrangements that let a small force do a big job. A crowbar is a lever: push down a little at one end, and you lift a heavy thing at the other. Bikes have gears so you don’t have to pedal hard up a hill.

Topic 5

Earth & Space

The Sun, the planets, day and night.

Step outside on a clear night. All those tiny dots up there are stars — huge balls of glowing hot gas, much bigger than Earth, just impossibly far away. Our Sun is a star too. It only looks so big and bright because it’s the closest one to us.

The Sun has eight planets orbiting around it, and together they make up our Solar System. In order from the Sun outwards:

Mercury · Venus · Earth · Mars · Jupiter · Saturn · Uranus · Neptune

Memory trick: “My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming.” The first letter of each word matches a planet in order.

Here’s how day and night work. The Earth spins on its axis, like a spinning top. One full spin takes 24 hours — that’s a day. As the Earth spins, half of it faces the Sun (that half is experiencing daytime) and the other half faces away (that half is in night). When your side is facing the Sun, we call it day; when it’s facing away, night. The Sun doesn’t “set” — the Earth turns you away from it.

The Earth also orbits (travels in a big circle around) the Sun. One full orbit takes 365 and a quarter days — that’s a year. The little quarter day is why every four years we add an extra day (the 29th of February): a leap year.

Finally, the Moon orbits the Earth, taking about 28 days to go all the way around. That’s roughly a month.
