Topic 1

Our Five Senses

Our body has five amazing ways to find out about the world.

Every single thing you know about the world around you comes in through your senses. Stop and think about that for a moment. The colour of the sky, the smell of dinner cooking, the sound of your own name — all of it comes from your senses.

Humans have five main senses, and each one uses a different part of the body to work:

  • Sight — we see with our eyes. Light bounces off things and into our eyes, and our brain turns it into a picture.
  • Hearing — we hear with our ears. Sounds are tiny wiggles in the air that our ears catch.
  • Smell — we smell with our nose. Tiny bits of smell float in the air.
  • Taste — we taste with our tongue. Our tongue can tell sweet, salty, sour, and bitter.
  • Touch — we feel with our skin. Skin can feel soft, sharp, hot, cold, and wet.

Lots of animals have extra-powerful senses. Eagles can see tiny mice from far away. Dogs can smell things humans can’t smell at all. Bats use sound to “see” in the dark. Your senses are amazing, but nature is full of creatures with even stronger ones.

Topic 2

Parts of the Body

The main parts of your amazing body.

Your body is made up of lots of different parts, and each one has a job. Some parts you can see from the outside. Some are hidden away inside.

The main outside parts of your body:

head · neck · shoulder · arm · elbow · hand · finger · chest · tummy · back · hip · leg · knee · foot · toe

Your head has lots of useful bits: your eyes to see, your ears to hear, your nose to smell, your mouth to eat and speak, and your teeth to chew. Inside your head is your brain, which does all the thinking and remembering.

Inside your chest live your heart (which pumps blood all around your body) and your lungs (which take in air when you breathe). In your tummy is your stomach, where your food goes.

Try this: hold your hand against your chest and feel your heart thumping. It’s working all day and all night, even when you’re asleep. It never stops. Amazing, isn’t it?

Topic 3

Animals

Animals come in all shapes and sizes.

There are millions of different kinds of animals on Earth, from tiny insects you can hardly see to whales bigger than a bus. Scientists like to sort them into groups based on what they have in common.

In Year 1 we meet the main groups:

  • Birds have feathers and wings, and they lay eggs. Most fly, but some (like penguins) don’t. Examples: robin, pigeon, chicken, eagle, owl.
  • Fish live in water and have scales. They breathe using gills. Examples: goldfish, shark, trout, seahorse.
  • Mammals have fur or hair and feed their babies with milk. Examples: dog, cat, cow, mouse, lion. You are a mammal too!
  • Reptiles have scaly skin and most lay eggs. Examples: snake, lizard, turtle, crocodile.
  • Amphibians start life in water and later live on land too. Examples: frog, toad, newt.
  • Insects have six legs and their bodies are in three parts. Examples: ant, bee, ladybird, butterfly.

The best way to learn the groups is to watch. When you spot an animal, ask: how many legs? Fur or feathers or scales? Lives on land or in water? With a bit of practice, sorting animals becomes easy.

Topic 4

Plants

Plants grow all around us.

Plants are living things, just like animals, but they’re very different from us. They don’t move about or eat sandwiches. They stay in one spot and they make their own food from sunlight.

Most plants have these four parts:

  • Roots — the bits that reach down into the soil. Roots hold the plant in place and drink up water.
  • Stem — the tube that holds the plant up straight. Water travels up the stem to reach the leaves.
  • Leaves — these catch sunlight. A plant uses sunlight to make its own food. This is called photosynthesis, a tricky word we’ll learn properly later.
  • Flower — the pretty coloured part. Flowers turn into fruit, and the fruit holds new seeds that will grow into brand new plants.

Plants give us LOADS of the food we eat. Carrots are actually roots. Lettuce is leaves. Broccoli is flowers. Apples and strawberries are fruit. Next time you eat, have a look: which bit of a plant are you eating?

Topic 5

The Four Seasons

The year rolls around in four parts.

Here in the UK, the weather changes through the year. Some months are hot and sunny, some are cold and snowy. These four patterns repeat every year in the same order, and we call them seasons.

Spring (March, April, May). The cold starts to fade. Flowers open. Baby animals are born. Birds sing. The days get longer.

Summer (June, July, August). Hot and sunny. The longest days of the year are in summer. Great for ice-creams and playing outside.

Autumn (September, October, November). The weather cools. Leaves on the trees change colour — red, orange, yellow, brown — and fall to the ground. Hedgehogs get ready to hibernate.

Winter (December, January, February). Cold, and sometimes snowy. The days are short. The trees have no leaves. Many animals hide away.

The seasons happen because the Earth tilts as it moves around the Sun. You’ll learn about that properly in a few years. For now, just notice: every three months, the whole world around you changes colour.
