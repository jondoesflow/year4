Topic 1

World War II (1939 – 1945)

The biggest war in human history.

World War II began on 1 September 1939, when Germany (under its dictator Adolf Hitler) invaded Poland. Britain and France, who had promised to defend Poland, declared war two days later. Over the next six years, the war would pull in nearly every country on Earth and kill an estimated 70–85 million people. It was the most destructive conflict in human history.

The main sides:

  • Allies: UK, France, USSR (Soviet Union), USA (from 1941), China, Canada, Australia, and many more.
  • Axis: Germany, Italy, Japan.

Key moments and dates:

  • 10 May 1940 — Winston Churchill becomes UK Prime Minister. His stirring speeches (“We shall fight on the beaches…”) kept morale high through the darkest times.
  • Summer 1940 — the Battle of Britain. The RAF (Royal Air Force) fought off the German Luftwaffe in the skies over southern England. If they’d lost, Germany would probably have invaded Britain.
  • 7 December 1941 — Japan attacks the American naval base at Pearl Harbour in Hawaii. The USA joins the war.
  • 6 June 1944 (D-Day) — Allied forces land in Normandy, France. The biggest seaborne invasion in history. The long push across Europe begins.
  • 8 May 1945 (VE Day) — Victory in Europe. Germany surrenders. Huge street parties in Britain.
  • 15 August 1945 (VJ Day) — Japan surrenders, ending the war completely.

Life on the British home front:

  • Blackouts — all lights had to be switched off or covered at night so German bombers couldn’t see cities.
  • Rationing — every person got a ration book. Food, clothes and fuel were all limited. Adults got tiny amounts of butter, sugar, meat. Rationing didn’t end until 1954.
  • Evacuation — hundreds of thousands of children were sent from cities (which were being bombed) to live with strangers in the countryside. Many had never left London before.
  • The Blitz — heavy German bombing of British cities from September 1940. Londoners slept in Underground stations for safety.
Topic 2

The Tudors (1485 – 1603)

Henry VIII’s six wives and Elizabeth I’s Golden Age.

The Tudor period in England ran from 1485 to 1603 — 118 years and only five monarchs. It’s one of the most dramatic chapters in English history, full of wars, weddings, executions, religious revolution and the birth of English theatre.

The period started when Henry VII killed Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485, ending the Wars of the Roses and founding the Tudor dynasty.

The superstar Tudor is his son, Henry VIII (king 1509–1547). Henry is most famous for his six wives. The classroom mnemonic for their fates is: “divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived”. The wives, in order: Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr.

When Henry’s first wife (Catherine of Aragon) didn’t give him a son, he asked the Catholic Pope for permission to divorce her. The Pope refused. In 1534, Henry broke away from the Catholic Church and declared himself head of a brand new Church of England. This massive event is called the English Reformation, and it changed religion in Britain forever.

Henry’s daughter by his second wife became the most celebrated English monarch of them all: Elizabeth I, who ruled from 1558 to 1603. Her reign is called the Elizabethan Golden Age:

  • William Shakespeare was writing his greatest plays (Romeo and Juliet, Hamlet, Macbeth, A Midsummer Night’s Dream). The Globe Theatre opened in London.
  • Francis Drake sailed around the world (1577–1580) — the first English captain to do it.
  • In 1588, the Spanish Armada — a huge fleet of 130 ships sent by Philip II of Spain to invade England — was defeated by the English navy and storms in the Channel. It was a huge national moment.
  • Elizabeth never married. She’s sometimes called “the Virgin Queen”. When she died in 1603, the Tudor dynasty ended.
Topic 3

Local History

The story of your own town.

The history you’ve learned is the big, famous version. But every single town, village and city has its own history, layered on like pages of a book. A local history study asks a simple question: how has THIS place changed over time?

Year 6 historians investigate a local place using primary sources (documents from the time) and secondary sources (later writing about it). Sources to try:

  • Old maps. A map from 1900 compared to today’s map can tell you a lot. Fields that became estates. Railways that vanished. Rivers that got built over.
  • Census records. A census is a snapshot of who lived in each household every 10 years. You can look up who lived in your house in 1911 and what their job was.
  • Photographs. How has the high street changed? What shops were there 80 years ago?
  • The oldest building. Often a church or a pub. Old buildings survive because they were built well — and they anchor the story of a place.
  • Place names. Tell you who settled there centuries ago. -by or -thorpe (Derby, Grimsby, Scunthorpe) = Viking. -chester or -caster (Chester, Lancaster) = Roman fort. -ham or -ton (Birmingham, Brighton) = Anglo-Saxon farm/village.
  • War memorials. Every village has one. The names on them tell you who fought and died.

Oral history is brilliant too: talking to grandparents and great-grandparents. They remember things that aren’t in any book.

Great local history projects include: map your town across the decades; tell the story of the oldest building; find out who lived in your house 100 years ago.

Topic 4

A Theme Through British History

E.g. how crime and punishment changed.

Instead of walking through British history chronologically, Year 6 often picks a single theme and traces it through the whole period. One of the most vivid themes to follow is crime and punishment. Over 2,000 years, the way we deal with wrongdoing has changed almost beyond recognition.

  • Roman Britain (43 AD – 410 AD). Romans wrote down their laws and enforced them harshly. Criminals might be whipped, enslaved, or executed. Crucifixion was a very public punishment used for slaves and rebels.
  • Anglo-Saxon Britain. A fairer system called wergild (“man-price”) appeared. Injured someone? You paid their wergild. Killed someone? You paid a much bigger one. The system reduced endless feuds between families.
  • Medieval England. Very superstitious. People believed God would help prove guilt. Trial by ordeal might mean putting your hand into boiling water and, if the burn healed cleanly after three days, you were innocent (decided by a priest). Public punishments were normal: the stocks and the pillory.
  • Tudor and Stuart England (1485–1714). Harsh and public. Hanging was the main punishment. Witchcraft trials became a panic, especially under King James I — thousands were hanged.
  • Victorian England (1837–1901). The first proper prisons were built, shifting from killing criminals to locking them up. Transportation sent convicts in prison ships to Australia. The first professional police force (“bobbies”, named after Sir Robert Peel) appeared in 1829.
  • Today. Prisons, fines, community service, tags. The death penalty was abolished in 1965. We now focus on rehabilitation as much as punishment.

Tracing the theme shows you how society gradually became more humane, more scientific, and less superstitious. It also shows that “normal” changes dramatically with time.

Topic 5

Grand Timeline of Britain

Putting it all in order.

By the end of Year 6 you’ll know enough about British history to put the pieces into one big timeline. Here it is:

  • 43 AD — Roman invasion under Emperor Claudius.
  • ~410 AD — Romans leave.
  • 450–600 AD — Anglo-Saxon settlement.
  • 793–878 AD — Viking raids and Danelaw.
  • 1066 — Normans invade (Battle of Hastings).
  • 1215 — Magna Carta signed by King John — a foundation of English law.
  • 1348–1350 — the Black Death kills roughly a third of England’s population.
  • 1485–1603 — Tudors. Henry VIII, Elizabeth I, Shakespeare, Armada.
  • 1603–1714 — Stuarts. English Civil War (1642–51). Great Fire of London (1666).
  • 1714–1837 — Georgian era. Industrial Revolution begins late-1700s.
  • 1837–1901 — Victorian era. British Empire at its peak. Railways, steamships, factories.
  • 1914–1918 — World War I.
  • 1939–1945 — World War II.
  • 1948 — NHS founded.
  • 1952–2022 — Reign of Elizabeth II.
  • 2022 – present — Reign of Charles III.

Patterns worth noticing:

  • Invasions (Romans, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, Normans) each reshaped the country.
  • The Industrial Revolution (late 1700s – 1800s) — Britain went from a farming country to a factory country. Towns exploded in size. Railways linked everywhere. It changed everything about ordinary life.
  • Two world wars in the 20th century shaped the modern world.
