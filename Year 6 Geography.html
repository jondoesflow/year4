Topic 1

The World’s Countries & Continents

The whole map.

The world has around 195 countries, spread across seven continents. “Around” because the number depends on exactly what you count as a country (some places are disputed).

The biggest countries by land area:

  1. Russia — by far the biggest country. It stretches across most of northern Asia and into Europe, covering 11 time zones.
  2. Canada
  3. USA
  4. China
  5. Brazil

The biggest countries by population:

  1. India (as of 2023, now the most populous country)
  2. China
  3. USA
  4. Indonesia
  5. Pakistan

The world’s biggest cities are astonishingly huge. Tokyo (Japan) has roughly 37 million people — more than half the population of the entire UK. Other giant cities include Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, Mumbai, Beijing, Dhaka, and New York.

Topic 2

Latitude & Longitude

The grid that covers the Earth.

Every single place on Earth has a precise address made of two numbers: its latitude and its longitude. Together these form an invisible grid covering the whole planet.

Latitude lines run around the Earth east-west (horizontally when you look at a globe). They are parallel to each other and to the Equator, which sits at 0° latitude and circles the exact middle of the Earth. Latitude is measured in degrees NORTH or SOUTH of the Equator. The North Pole is at 90° N. The South Pole is at 90° S.

Longitude lines run from North Pole to South Pole — they’re north-south, vertically. The Prime Meridian (0° longitude) by international agreement passes through the town of Greenwich in London. Longitude is measured in degrees EAST or WEST of this line, up to 180°.

Every place on Earth has a coordinate. Some rough examples to know:

  • London: 51° N, 0° W (near the Prime Meridian)
  • New York: 41° N, 74° W
  • Tokyo: 36° N, 140° E
  • Sydney: 34° S, 151° E

Some important lines of latitude that you’ll see on a world map:

  • Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N) and Tropic of Capricorn (23.5° S) — the boundaries of the tropics. The Sun can be directly overhead between these two lines.
  • Arctic Circle (66.5° N) and Antarctic Circle (66.5° S) — inside these circles, there’s at least one day a year when the sun never sets (midsummer) and one when it never rises (midwinter).
Topic 3

Time Zones

Why lunch in London is bedtime in Sydney.

Because the Earth spins, only half of it faces the Sun at any given moment. The half facing the Sun has daytime; the half facing away has night. So noon (the Sun highest in the sky) happens at different MOMENTS in different places.

To keep things practical, the world is split into roughly 24 time zones, one for every hour of a 24-hour day. Each time zone is about 15 degrees of longitude wide. The UK’s time zone is Greenwich Mean Time (GMT), because of that Prime Meridian running through Greenwich. (In summer the UK shifts to BST, British Summer Time, one hour ahead.)

Rough time differences from London:

  • New York: 5 hours behind London.
  • Los Angeles: 8 hours behind.
  • Cairo: 2 hours ahead.
  • Dubai: 4 hours ahead.
  • India: 5 and a half hours ahead (yes, half hours exist in time zones!)
  • Bangkok: 7 hours ahead.
  • Tokyo: 9 hours ahead.
  • Sydney: 10 hours ahead.

So if it’s noon (12:00) in London, it’s 7:00 AM in New York and 10:00 PM in Sydney. Your family in Australia is about to go to bed when you’re eating lunch.

At 180° longitude (the opposite side of the Earth from Greenwich) sits the International Date Line. Cross it, and the date changes. It’s why you can fly from Tokyo to Hawaii and arrive the day before you left.

Topic 4

Economic Activity

How countries make money.

Every job produces something or provides something. Geographers group jobs into four sectors of economic activity. Understanding them helps you understand any country’s economy.

  • Primary sector — getting raw materials straight from the Earth. Farming, fishing, forestry, mining, quarrying, oil drilling. This was most people’s work for thousands of years.
  • Secondary sector — making things (manufacturing). Taking raw materials and turning them into products. Car factories, clothing workshops, bakeries, steel mills, electronics assembly.
  • Tertiary sector — providing services. Teaching, nursing, law, banking, shops, restaurants, hotels, hairdressers, bus drivers, tourism. This is the biggest sector in the UK today.
  • Quaternary sector — knowledge work. Scientists, software engineers, researchers, consultants, university professors. This sector has grown enormously in the last 50 years.

Countries have different economic profiles:

  • Wealthier developed countries (UK, Japan, USA) have lots of tertiary and quaternary jobs, fewer primary. Very few Brits work on farms nowadays.
  • Developing countries often have more primary and secondary sector work. Farming might employ half the population.
  • Oil-rich countries (Saudi Arabia, Norway) make most of their money from primary activity (oil), but they use the income to build tertiary and quaternary sectors.

Over your lifetime, many jobs will move from secondary (factories) into quaternary (knowledge work). Robots and machines keep taking over physical work. That’s why your education matters more than your grandparents’ did.

Topic 5

Fieldwork Skills

Being a real geographer.

All the geography you’ve learned so far has been theory. Fieldwork is when you leave the classroom and investigate real places with real tools. It’s what actual geographers do for a living.

Skills and tools you’ll use:

  • Compass — finds direction using the Earth’s magnetic field. Points to magnetic North. The eight-point compass: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW.
  • Ordnance Survey (OS) maps — Britain’s detailed topographic maps. They show every contour line, footpath, village, wood, church and pub. The standard scales are 1:25,000 (brilliant for walking) and 1:50,000 (for longer journeys).
  • Grid references — a way of pinpointing a location on an OS map. A four-figure grid reference pinpoints a 1 km square. A six-figure grid reference pinpoints roughly a 100 m square. Always read the numbers along the bottom first, then up the side — “along the corridor, up the stairs”.
  • Surveys and questionnaires — collect information from real people. You might count traffic, time how busy a shop gets, or ask local residents their opinions.
  • Sketch maps and labelled photographs — quick drawings and annotated photos record what you find.
  • Measuring tools — a tape measure for distances, a thermometer for temperature, a rain gauge for rainfall.

A good fieldwork project asks a question, collects evidence to answer it, and presents the findings. For example: “Is our High Street getting busier or quieter?” You’d count footfall at different times, compare to older data, and draw graphs.

Fieldwork can take you outside in any weather. Waterproofs and sturdy shoes are wise. Real geographers get a bit muddy.
