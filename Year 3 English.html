Topic 1

Prefixes & Suffixes

Word-parts stuck on the start or end.

English is a wonderfully Lego-like language. Words can be built up by sticking small pieces on the start or the end. Once you know what these pieces mean, you can work out words you’ve never seen before.

A prefix is a small word-part added to the start of a word. It changes the word’s meaning. The most important Year 3 prefixes:

  • un- means not — happy becomes unhappy, kind becomes unkind, safe becomes unsafe.
  • dis- also means not or the opposite of — agree becomes disagree, appear becomes disappear.
  • re- means again — do becomes redo, write becomes rewrite, fill becomes refill.
  • pre- means before — heat becomes preheat the oven, view becomes preview.
  • sub- means under — marine becomes submarine (an underwater boat), way becomes subway (an underground path).
  • super- means above or best — market becomes supermarket, star becomes superstar.

A suffix goes on the end of a word. Common suffixes include -ly (quick → quickly), -ful (care → careful, meaning “full of care”), -less (care → careless, meaning “without care”), -ness (kind → kindness), and -ment (move → movement).

Try spotting prefixes and suffixes when you read. Once you see them, you start seeing them everywhere.

Topic 2

Paragraphs

Grouping ideas together.

Have you ever tried to read a huge wall of writing with no breaks? It makes your eyes go funny. Your brain can’t find anywhere to pause. Good writers break their work up into paragraphs to give the reader chances to breathe.

A paragraph is a group of sentences all about one idea. When the idea changes, you start a new paragraph. Think of each paragraph as a little room in your writing. Each room has its own purpose.

Every new paragraph starts on a new line. In printed books, there’s often a small indent (the first word is pushed in a bit). In emails and websites, there’s usually a blank line between paragraphs instead.

Good moments to start a new paragraph:

  • When you start describing a new place.
  • When a new character speaks.
  • When time passes (“the next morning…”).
  • When you change from one topic to another.
Topic 3

Speech Marks

Showing what someone says.

When you write a story with characters in it, sooner or later they’re going to talk. But how does the reader know which bits are things characters SAY, and which bits are just description? That’s what speech marks (also called inverted commas) are for.

You wrap the spoken words in two little marks: “ at the start and ” at the end. Like a pair of gates keeping the speech inside.

“I am hungry,” said Mia.

Rules to remember:

  • The first word inside the speech marks has a capital letter.
  • The punctuation (comma, full stop, question mark, exclamation mark) goes inside the closing speech mark.
  • If the speech comes first and then you say who said it, use a comma (not a full stop) before the closing speech mark: “I am hungry,” said Mia.
  • If the saying comes first, use a comma after “said”: Mia said, “I am hungry.”
  • Every time a new person starts speaking, start a new line.

It looks fussy at first, but once you’ve done it a few times it becomes automatic.

Topic 4

Expanded Noun Phrases

Adding juicy words to a noun.

A plain noun is a bit boring. “The dog.” It tells us something, but not much. Year 3 teaches us how to expand noun phrases by adding describing words until the picture in the reader’s head is crystal clear.

Start with a noun: dog.

Add one adjective: the scruffy dog. Already we know more.

Add more adjectives: the scruffy brown dog. Now we can picture it.

Add a phrase with more detail: the scruffy brown dog with the wagging tail. Brilliant.

Here are a few more examples to inspire you:

  • the huge old stone castle at the end of the hill
  • a bright red shiny apple
  • a tiny, squeaky little mouse with twitchy whiskers

Be careful — too many adjectives and your writing sounds over-the-top. Two or three well-chosen ones are better than five or six squashed together.

Topic 5

Poetry

Playing with the sound and shape of words.

A poem is writing where sound matters as much as meaning. Poets don’t just pick words because they fit the story — they pick them because of how they sound, how they feel, and the pictures they make in your head.

Two tools poets love:

  • Rhyme — words that sound the same at the end. cat / hat / bat / flat / mat. Rhyme makes lines of poetry fit together musically. Lots of nursery rhymes rhyme: Jack and Jill went up the HILL, to fetch a pail of WATER. Hmm, those don’t actually rhyme. But you know what I mean!
  • Alliteration — words that start with the same sound, one after another: silly slippery snakes, big bright buttons, cold crunchy crisps. It’s fun to say out loud.

Poems can be funny, sad, silly or serious. They don’t have to rhyme. Some great poems don’t rhyme at all — they use rhythm and pictures instead. Try reading a few out loud. Poetry isn’t really finished until it’s been spoken.

Try writing a poem of your own. Pick something you love — your cat, the sea, a favourite food — and jot down all the words that describe it. Then play with the order. You’ve written a poem.

Topic 6

Year 3 Tricky Words

Words to learn off by heart.

English is full of words that don’t follow the normal rules. You can’t work them out from the sounds alone — you just have to learn them. The best way is to see them a lot and to write them a lot. These are some of the Year 3 tricky words.

accident, breath, build, believe, caught, century, circle, different, earth, eight, famous, favourite, forward, fruit, group, heart, heard, increase, island, learn, length, medicine, natural, often, probably, purpose, quarter, question, regular, remember, straight, thought, through

Some tricks for learning them:

  • Write each one out three times.
  • Say the letters out loud as you write.
  • Spot them in books while you’re reading.
  • Make up a silly rhyme. “Big elephants can always understand small elephants” spells BECAUSE.
